    Section 7.1 Fraction Basics

    Before we get into operations with fractions, there are some basics of fractions that we will study.
    🔗

    Subsection 7.1.1 Fractions and Units

    Until the abstract concept of a number is understood, it is always advantageous to think of a number with units (5 toys, 6 miles, 3 ounces) and with fractions this is no different. In fact, we will use units to emphasize the notion of a fraction. Consider 2/3 of a foot.
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2}{3} = \frac{\text{numerator}}{\text{denominator}} \end{equation*}
    where \(3\) is the number of parts one the whole (1 foot) and the numerator (2) is the number of the fractional parts.
    🔗
    If we have an understanding of a third, then \(2/3\) is 2 ’’thirds’’ and we can handle it
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.1.2 Fraction Models

    As with other numbers and operations in mathematics, models are very important in understanding fractions. Models then help with operations involving fractions.
    🔗
    One pitfall is that the models for fractions are often very similar to those of multiplication and division and so it is important to be very clear about what is being asked for.
    🔗
    Area or regional model
    🔗
    The following models are useful for describing fractions. The shaded area relative to the total area represents the fraction. In each case, the fraction is 3/4.
    🔗
    Linear Measurement Model
    🔗
    This uses the number line. This is helpful for word problems with length or distance for example.
    🔗
    Set Model
    🔗
    A set model can be used as long as it is clear what is asked. In the following, if we have red and blue marbles and ask what fraction are red, you can see that the picture represents 3/5. Be careful with this, because we often use the same model for either multiplication ( \(4 \times 5\) ) or division ( \(20 \div 5\) ).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.1.3 Ordering of Fractions

    • In counting by fractional units ( “one-fourth, two-fourths, three-fourths, …” ) they count both verbally and in writing.
      🔗
      🔗
    • In ordering (which in general is saved until later), we do two easier concepts:
      • Ordering with the same denominator. This shows that \(\displaystyle \frac{3}{4} \gt \frac{1}{4}\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • If you have the same numerator, then the fraction with the smaller denominator is larger. The model below shows that \(\displaystyle \frac{2}{5} > \frac{2}{7}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      • Using an intermediate fraction: Compare \(\frac{3}{5}\) and \(\frac{2}{7}\text{.}\) Since neither the numerators or denominators are equal, we can’t use the above methods, but we know that \(\frac{3}{5} > \frac{1}{2}\) and \(\frac{1}{2} > \frac{2}{7}\text{,}\) so \(\frac{3}{5} > \frac{2}{7}\text{.}\)
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.1.4 Fraction Equivalents

    The next big understanding with fractions is to note that many different fractions represent the same value. For example \(\frac{2}{3}\) can be written as \(\frac{4}{6}, \frac{6}{9}, \frac{8}{12}, \ldots\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The following two models represents \(\frac{2}{3}\) in multiple ways:
    🔗
    • A fractional strip:
      🔗
      🔗
    • A subdivided area (rectangular model):
      🔗
      🔗
    • Numbers: Once you understand the above pictures, you can fill in the missing values:
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \frac{2}{3} \amp = \frac{ \boxed{\phantom{2}}}{6} \amp \frac{4}{7} \amp = \frac{ \boxed{\phantom{2}}}{21} \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    We summarize the results of the above models with the following:
    🔗

    Property 7.1.1. Equivalent Fractions.

    Let \(a,b\) and \(n\) be positive whole numbers. Then
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a}{b}= \frac{na}{nb} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    We will see that the two most important reasons for understanding equivalent fractions are
    • Finding a common denominator for addition and subtraction of fractions.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Reducing a fraction to a simpler form.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    We will see the first item here in the next section of the text, But the following shows a few examples on how to reduce a fraction or shows that a fraction is in reduced form.
    🔗

    Lemma 7.1.2. Fraction in Simple Form.

    A Fraction in Reduce or Simplest Form A fraction \(\dfrac{a}{b}\) is in reduced form or simplest form if
    \begin{equation*} GCF(a,b)=1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This is a rule that can check if a fraction is in simplest form. If a fraction is not in reduced form and \(GCF(a,b)=k\text{,}\) then factoring \(k\) out of the numerator and denominator will result in a fraction in reduced form.
    🔗

    Example 7.1.3.

    Determine if the following fractions are in reduced form.
    🔗
    (a)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{144}{195} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The prime factorization of 144 is \(2^4 \cdot 3^2\) and the prime factorization of 195 is \(3 \cdot 5 \cdot 13\text{.}\) Since they have a common factor of 3, then \(GCD(144,195)=3\text{,}\) so this is not in reduced form.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{53}{96} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    In this case, since 53 is a prime number, then \(GCD(53,96)=1\) and this is in reduced form.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We now show some examples that if \(GCD(a,b) > 1\text{,}\) how to reduce the fraction. That is if we have a fraction in the form
    \begin{equation*} \frac{ka}{kb} \end{equation*}
    that we reduce it to
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a}{b}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 7.1.4.

    Reduce the following fractions to simplest form:
    🔗
    (a)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{20}{45} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    In this case, the numerator and denominator are small, so noticing a common factor is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{20}{45}= \frac{5\cdot 4}{5 \cdot 9}= \frac{4}{9} \end{equation*}
    where the fraction on the right is in simplest form because there is no other factor (other than 1) between 4 and 9.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{144}{195} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    As we saw in the example above,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 144 \amp = 2^4 \cdot 3^2 \amp 195 \amp = 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 17 \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    So we can write the original fraction as
    \begin{equation*} \frac{144}{195} = \frac{2^4 \cdot 3^2}{3 \cdot 5 \cdot 17} \end{equation*}
    and there is a common value \(k=3\text{,}\) so we write this as
    \begin{equation*} \frac{144}{195} = \frac{2^4 \cdot 3}{5 \cdot 17} = \frac{16 \cdot 3}{85} = \frac{48}{85} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We can also use equivalent fractions for the following problem in which we compare two fractions.
    🔗

    Example 7.1.5. Comparing Fractions.

    Which of the following is larger: \(\dfrac{3}{7}\) or \(\dfrac{4}{9}\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We saw above that it is relatively straightforward to determine which of two fractions is larger if the two denominators are the same or if the two numerators are the same. This example doesn’t fit either of these. We’ll use equivalent fractions to determine this.
    🔗
    We’ll make the two denominators the same by multiplying the first by the factor \(9/9\) and the second by \(7/7\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \frac{3}{7} \amp = \frac{3 \cdot 9}{7 \cdot 9}= \frac{27}{63} \amp \frac{4}{9} \amp = \frac{4 \cdot 7}{9 \cdot 7}= \frac{28}{63} \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    and now it is clear that the one with the larger numerator is the larger fraction so
    \begin{equation*} \frac{4}{9} > \frac{3}{7} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.1.5 Improper Fractions and Mixed Numbers

    "How much sliced cheese would you like," asks the person behind the deli counter? "A pound and a quarter" you say.
    🔗
    The fraction in the exchange above is a mixed number and often write it as \(1 \frac{1}{4}\) and this is used in many situations. Below we define this as well as "regular" fractions which are either proper or improper.
    🔗

    Definition 7.1.6.

    Mixed Numbers, Improper and Proper Fractions If \(a\) and \(b\) are whole numbers, then
    • A mixed number is a fraction expressed as the sum of a whole number and a fraction.
      🔗
      🔗
    • An improper fraction is a fraction \(\frac{a}{b}\) with \(a \geq b\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • A proper fraction is a fraction \(\frac{a}{b}\) with \(a<b\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.1.7. Proper and Improper Fractions and Mixed Numbers.

    • The number \(2 \frac{1}{3}\) is a mixed number and is shorthand notation for \(2 + \frac{1}{3}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The number \(\frac{13}{6}\) is an improper fraction because the top is greater than the bottom.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The number \(\frac{65}{101}\) is a proper fraction because the top is smaller than the bottom.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Converting between mixed numbers and improper fractions.

    Example 7.1.8.
    Use an area model to convert \(2 \frac{1}{3}\) to an improper fraction.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For this, we’ll use circles, but other shapes work the same. Draw two full circles plus a third of another:
    🔗
    And each whole can be subdivided into 3 pieces to get
    🔗
    This shows that \(2\frac{1}{3}\) is equal to \(\frac{7}{3}\text{.}\) This can be done arithmetically by \(2 \cdot 3 +1=7\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} 2\frac{1}{3}= \frac{7}{3} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Example 7.1.9.
    Use long division to write \(\frac{13}{6}\) as a mixed number. Since \(13 \div 6 = 2 R1\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \frac{13}{6}= 2 \frac{1}{6} \end{equation*}

    Subsection 7.1.6 Teachers’ Solutions

    Word problems are key to understand fractions. We use fractions in common language and its helpful to show students how we use fractions in this context.
    🔗
    Any type of model that we have shown in this section can be used in a Teacher’s Solution and it’s easiest to see this with specific questions.
    🔗

    Example 7.1.10.

    Produce a Teacher’s Solution on the following problem: Jack drank \(\frac{2}{5}\) of a carton of orange juice. His sister Sarah drank \(\frac{1}{5}\) of the carton. How much of the carton did they drink altogether?
    🔗
    Solution.
    We can use any type of model, but an area model may be most helpful. We will use a rectangle one (since this looks like a carton) with vertical slices.
    🔗
    And the total area shaded is \(3/5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Jack and Sarah together drank \(\frac{3}{5}\) of the carton of orange juice.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.1.11.

    Produce a Teacher’s Solution on the following problem: Derek had 9 bananas. He ate \(\frac{1}{3}\) of them. How many bananas did he have left?
    🔗
    Solution.
    For this, let’s use a set model since bananas are discrete (we can count them). We could use pictures of bananas, but students who are working with fractions have some abstract skills and we’ll just use circles.
    🔗
    The bananas that he ate are shaded red and those he didn’t each, are shaded blue.
    🔗
    He has 6 bananas left.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    As word problems get more difficult, the needed diagrams get more difficult as well. The following shows an example of a two-step word problem.
    🔗

    Example 7.1.12.

    Develop a Teacher’s Solution to the following: Mrs. Murphy has a bowl of 300 marbles. \(\frac{2}{5}\) of them are red and the rest are blue. She handed out \(\frac{3}{4}\) of the red marbles. How many marbles are left in the bowl?
    🔗
    Solution.
    As stated above, this is a two-step word problem. One way to solve this is to first find the number of red and blue marbles, then determine the number handed out and from that, the number left can be determined. We can represent this with a two-step diagram as follows:
    🔗
    In the top diagram, because the fraction of marbles in the bowl is given in fifths, we divide the bar into 5 equal pieces. Each square in the top is \(300\div 5 = 60\) marbles so there are two squares worth of red marbles or \(2 \times 60 = 120\) red marbles.
    🔗
    The second step is only in terms of the red marbles, so we use an arrow to denote that the red marbles in the top is the same as that in the bottom diagram. Because in the second step, there is \(\frac{3}{4}\text{,}\) we divide the bottom diagram in 4 equal pieces. The 4 squares in the bottom diagram represents 120 marbles, so each square in the bottom diagram is \(120 \div 4 = 30\) marbles. The 3 shaded is \(3 \times 30 = 90\) marbles. and the red marbles left is one square or 30 marbles.
    🔗
    There were \(3 \times 60 = 180\) blue marbles in the diagram and together with the 30 red marbles, means there are \(180+30=210\) marbles left in the bowl.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The last example was a bit more complicated and we’ll see more like this later in the text. We will use this technique of dividing a bar into sections in other fraction problems as well as ratio and proportions in Chapter 8.
    🔗
    🔗
