    Section 6.5 Prime Number and Divisibility Proofs

    This section dives deeper into many of the proofs in this chapter.
    🔗
    This Chapter covers more properties of numbers, specifically integers. In Chapter 2 many properties like the distributive and associative were seen. Technically, these are actually mathematical axioms or statements that we assume are true. We show that they hold true using diagrams, however there is no formal mathematical proof of these, but we assume them to hold.
    🔗
    This chapter is all about prime numbers and factoring whole numbers. And we will see in Chapter 7 that these play an important role for understanding fractions. We also want to build some theorems based on these ideas. As a review, formal mathematics has some key components:
    🔗
    Axioms
    🔗
    When constructing an argument, we use a sequence of known facts to make those arguments. Many times, these are properties of arithmetic like the distributive and commutative properties. Other times these are other theorems or lemmas that have been proven.
    🔗
    Definitions
    🔗
    Because of the precise nature of mathematics, defining our terms is crucial. We all need to agree that we are thinking of the same things.
    🔗
    Theorems and Lemmas
    🔗
    A theorem (important result) or a lemma (minor result) allows us to make a general statement. It is proved using mathematical facts and logic.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.5.1 The Divisibility Lemma

    Recall that the Lemma 6.2.5 take a number and splits it into the sum of two numbers to help determine if a number is divisible by another. The following is the proof of that lemma.
    🔗

    Proof of the Divisibility Lemma.
    First, we will show that if \(B\) is divisible by \(k\text{,}\) then \(A+B\) is divisible by \(k\text{.}\) We know that \(k\) divides \(A\text{,}\) so that means that \(A\) can be written \(A=a\cdot k\text{.}\) Also, since \(k\) divides \(B\text{,}\) that \(B\) can be written \(B=b\cdot k\text{.}\) We will see if \(k\) divides \(A+B\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}A+B\amp = a\cdot k + b\cdot k\amp \amp \text{by divisibility}\\\amp = (a+b) \cdot k\amp \amp \text{by distributive}\\\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This means that \(A+B\) is divisible by \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Next, we will show that if \(A+B\) is divisible by \(k\text{,}\) then \(B\) is divisible by \(k\text{.}\) Since \(A+B\) is divisible by \(k\text{,}\) then means that \(A+B\) can be written as \(A+B = c\cdot k\) for some whole number \(c\text{,}\) where \(c\) is larger than \(a\text{.}\) Next, we want to show that \(B\) is divisible by \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The number \(B\) can be written as
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}B\amp = (A+B) - A \\\amp = c \cdot k - a \cdot k \\\amp = (c-a) \cdot k\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    since \(c\) and \(a\) are whole numbers with \(c\) larger than \(a\text{,}\) then \(B\) is divisible by \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.5.2 Divisibility Test Proofs

    Next, we revisiting the divisibility tests of 3, 4, 9 and 11 that are stated in Theorem 6.2.8.
    🔗

    Proof of the Divisibility Test by 4.
    Let a whole number \(N\) be written as
    \begin{equation*} N = 100 a + b \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (why can it be written this way?) Since \(100a\) can be written as \(4 \cdot (25a)\text{,}\) it is divisible by 4. In addition, \(b\) is divisible by 2 if and only if \(b\) is divisible by 4. By the divisibility theorem, \(N\) is divisible by 2 if and only if \(100a\) and \(b\) are divisible by 4.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof of the Divisibility of a 3-digit number by 3 and 9.
    Let \(N\) be a three-digit number and let it be written:
    \begin{equation*} N = 100 a + 10 b + c. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Write \(100a=99a+a\) and \(10b=9b+b\) or
    \begin{equation*} N = 99a + 9b + (a+b+c) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The term \(99a=9b=9(11a+b)\) is divisible by both 3 and 9. By the Divisibility lemma, \(N\) is divisible by 3 (or 9) if and only if \(a+b+c\) is divisible by 3 (or 9).
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof of the divisibility test by 11.
    Consider a six-digit number (other sized numbers are similar), \(N\text{.}\) Write \(N\) as
    \begin{equation*} N = 100,000a + 10, 000 b + 1,000c + 100d + 10 e + f \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Rewrite \(N\) as follows:
    \begin{equation*} N = 100,001a + 1,001c + 11e + 9999b + 99d + (b+d+f) - (a+c+e) \end{equation*}
    which can be written as
    \begin{equation*} N = 11 (9091a+91c+e) + 11(9090b+9d) + (b+c+f)- (a+c+e) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    By the divisibility theorem, since the first two terms are divisible by 11, \(N\) is divisible by 11 if and only if
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}(b+c+f)-(a+c+e)\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    is divisible by 11, which is the same as the statement of the theorem.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.5.3 Prime Number Proofs

    Lemma 6.5.1.

    Every whole number \(N \gt 1\) is a multiple of a prime.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.5.2.

    Let’s try this with \(N=56\text{.}\) All of its factors are \(\{1,2,4,7,8,28,56\}\text{.}\) We note that 2 is a prime factor (also 7 is). We will use this in the proof below.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    List all of the factors of \(N\) and call \(p\) the smallest factor greater than \(1\text{.}\) We will show that \(p\) is prime and therefore \(N\) is a multiple of a prime.
    🔗
    This will be shown by a technique called proof by contradiction. In short, we make an assumption that contradicts what we are trying to show, resulting in an absurd statement, thus the original statement must be correct.
    🔗
    Assume that \(p\) is not prime. Then we can write \(p=rs\text{,}\) however both \(r<p\) and \(s<p\text{,}\) but this is not possible because \(p\) is the smallest factor greater than 1, which contradicts the fact that \(p\) is smallest factor.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    (of the fundamental theorem of arithmetic)
    🔗
    By Lemma 6.5.1, write
    \begin{equation*} N = p_{1} n_{1} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    where \(p_{1}\) is prime. If \(n_{1}=1\text{,}\) then \(N\) is prime and the factors are \(1\) and \(p_{1}\) and this is the unique result.
    🔗
    If \(n_{1}\) is not prime, then use Lemma 6.5.1 to write
    \begin{equation*} n_{1} = p_{2} n_{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    where \(p_{2}\) is prime and thus
    \begin{equation*} N = p_{1} p_{2} n_{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    if \(n_{2}=1\text{,}\) then the above is the prime factorization. If not, repeat these steps and write
    \begin{equation*} N = p_{1} p_{2} p_{3} \cdots p_{k} n_{k} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    with \(n_{k}=1\text{.}\) Since the numbers \(n_{1}, n_{2}, \ldots, n_{k}\) are always decreasing, this process must end. Thus we can write
    \begin{equation*} N = p_{1} p_{2} p_{3} \cdots p_{k} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    To prove that this is a unique factorization, we need a little more background and thus will see this in 5.5.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Of the Largest Factor Lemma.
    Let \(N=pn\text{,}\) where \(p\) is prime by the Smallest Factor Lemma. The Since \(p \leq n\) and therefore \(p^{2} \leq np=N\text{.}\) Thus \(p \leq \sqrt{N}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.5.4 How many primes are there?

    Theorem 6.5.3.

    There are an infinite number of prime numbers.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Assume that there are a finite number of primes and let \(P\) be the largest. Define the number \(N\) to be
    \begin{equation*} N = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdots P+1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since \(N\) is not divisible by any prime between 2 and \(P\text{,}\) it must also be prime and is obviously larger than \(P\text{,}\) so there’s a contradiction. Since this violates the assumption that there are a finite number and \(P\) is the largest, the result is that there must be an infinite number of primes.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.5.5 Proof by Contradiction

    The following steps shows the logic for proof by contradiction.
    🔗
    1. Either a statement or its negative is true, but not both.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Assume that the negative of a statement is true. Show how that assumption leads to a logical contradiction.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Since there is a contradiction, we conclude that the original assumption is incorrect, thus the original statement is true.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    In the proof above,
    🔗
    1. We started with the original statement that we were trying to prove, “There is an infinite number of prime numbers.”
      🔗
      🔗
    2. We assumed the negative of the statement: “There is a finite number of primes” and showed that this resulted in a prime larger than the largest of the finite number.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Since there is a contradiction, the original assumption that there is only a finite number of primes, leading to the result that there is an infinite number of primes.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.5.6 The Greatest Common Factors and Lowest Common Multiples

    Proof.
    (of Euclid’s Algorithm:) Assume that \(n\) is a common factor of both \(a\) and \(b\text{.}\) Since \(a=bq+r\text{,}\) and \(r=a-bq\text{,}\) then \(n\) also a common factor of \(r\text{.}\) Let \(N\) be the largest common factor of both then
    \begin{equation*} N = GCF(a,b) = GCF(b,r) \end{equation*}
