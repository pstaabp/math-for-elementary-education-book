    Section 6.4 Greatest Common Factors and Least Common Multiples

    Definition 6.4.1.

    The Greatest Common Factor of two whole numbers \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) written \(GCF(a,b)\text{,}\) is the largest whole number which is a factor of both \(a\) and \(b\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.4.2.

    Find \(GCF(24,60)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    We can list all of the factors of both 24 and 60:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}\text{Factors of 24:}\amp \qquad \qquad 1,2,3,4,6,8,12,24\\ \text{Factors of 60:}\amp \qquad \qquad 1,2,3,4,5,6,10,12,15,20,30,60\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    and looking at the list, the largest common factor is 12. Therefore \(GCF(24,60)=12\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.4.3.

    Calculate \(GCF(144,180)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    In this case, it awfully difficult to write down all of the factors. Instead, another technique is to write out the prime factorization (recall that to do this you can use the factor trees presented in ) of both:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}144\amp = 2\cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 \\ 180\amp = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 5\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    and looking at the common prime factors:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}GCF(144,180)\amp = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 36\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.4.4.

    Calculate \(GCF(2400,450)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    In this case, it awfully difficult to write down all of the factors. Instead, another technique is to write out the prime factorization of both:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}2400\amp = 2^{5} \cdot 3 \cdot 5^{2} \\ 450\amp = 2 \cdot 3^{2} \cdot 5^{2}\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    and looking at the common prime factors:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}GCF(2400,450)\amp = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5^{2} = 3 \cdot 50 = 150\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.4.1 Least Common Multiple

    Recall that to find the sum of two fractions, we need a common denominator. The reasons why and details are in Chapter 7, but consider
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}\frac{1}{18}+ \frac{5}{12}\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    One way to to get a common denominator is to just multiply the denominators, however, it generally desirable to have a smaller one. A nice denominator is the least common multiple or LCM. In the example above the least common multiple of 18 and 12 is 36. For example:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}\frac{1}{18}+ \frac{5}{12}\amp = \frac{2}{36}+ \frac{5\cdot 3}{36}= \frac{17}{36}\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Note: many people use the term least common denominator for the LCM because of this.
    🔗

    Definition 6.4.5.

    The Least Common Multiple of two nonzero whole numbers \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) written \(LCM(a,b)\) is the smallest whole number (other than zero) that is a multiple of both \(a\) and \(b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.4.6.

    Find
    • \(\displaystyle LCM(12,18)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle LCM(24,64)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    by writing down enough multiples until the smallest one is found.
    🔗
    Solution.
    • Write a few multiples of each:
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}\text{Multiples of 12}\amp \qquad \qquad 12,24,36,48,60,72, \ldots \\ \text{Multiples of 18}\amp \qquad \qquad 18,36,54,72, \dots\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      and since the smallest common multiple is 36, \(LCM(12,18)=36\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • And for \(LCM(24,64)\)
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}\text{Multiples of 24:}\amp \quad 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, 168, 192 \\ \text{Multiples of 64:}\amp \quad 64, 128, 192, 256,\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      and you can see that 192 is the smallest multiple common to both, so \(LCM(24,64)=192\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    There is a nice way to visualize the LCM using a number line. Consider \(LCM(24,30)\text{.}\) We need multiples of both numbers, which we can plot on a number line:
    🔗
    and the LCM will be the point on the number line where the hops first land on the same value.
    🔗
    Similar to the GCF, when the the numbers get larger, finding the LCM using this method is difficult and therefore, it’s easy to miss the lowest common multiple. We will, like we did for the GCF, use the prime factorization to find the LCM. The following is an example.
    🔗

    Example 6.4.7.

    Find \(LCM(72,150)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    In this case, we will write the prime factorization of both
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}72\amp = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 \\ 150\amp = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 5\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Note: to find these, you probably need to use a factor tree to find them.
    🔗
    A common multiple is to multiple all factors together, but the least one is the one in which the common factors are only written once:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}LCM(72,150)\amp = \underbrace{2 \cdot 3}_{\text{common factors}}\cdot \underbrace{2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3}_{\text{other factors of 72}}\cdot \underbrace{5 \cdot 5}_{\text{other factors of 150}}= 1800\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We can summarize finding the LCM and GCF as follows.
    🔗

    Note 6.4.8. .

    Consider two whole numbers \(m\) and \(n\) that can be written as their prime factorization:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}m\amp = 2^{i}3^{j} \cdots \\ n\amp = 2^{k}3^{\ell}\cdots \\\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    • To find the GCF, find the smaller power of all common terms.
      🔗
      🔗
    • To find the LCM, find the larger power of all common terms and include all terms that are in each number \(m\) and \(n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The following example shows this technique to find the LCM and GCF.
    🔗

    Example 6.4.9.

    Find both the LCM and GCF of \(80,36\) using this technique.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Use a factor tree, you can show that
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}80\amp = 2^{4} \cdot 5 \\ 36\amp = 2^{2} \cdot 3^{2}\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The GCF is the smallest power of all common terms. Since only powers of \(2\) are common then \(GCF(80,36) = 2^{2}=4\text{,}\) the smaller power of 2.
    🔗
    The LCM is the larger power of all terms, making sure that all terms are included:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}LCM(36,80)\amp = 2^{4} \cdot 3^{2} \cdot 5 = 720\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 6.4.1.1 Euclid’s Algorithm

    What if we need to find \(GCF(18000,96525)\text{?}\) Using the techniques above, we either 1) find all factors and then find the greatest common one or 2) find the prime factorization of each. In either case, this sounds ugly. Fortunately, there is another way called Euclid’s Algorithm that isn’t too difficult.
    🔗
    Lemma 6.4.10.
    Euclid’s Algorithm
    🔗
    If \(a = bq +r\) then \(GCF(a,b)=GCF(b,r)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    What this allows us to do is to reduce the size of the problem. As written \(a\) is the dividend, \(b\) is the divisor and \(r\) the remainder. Since \(b\) is less than \(a\) and \(r\) is less than \(b\) (why? Think Quotient-Remainder Theorem), the sizes of the number reduces when you do this.
    🔗
    To use Euclid’s algorithm, we will find \(r\text{,}\) the remainder of \(a \div b\) (or \(b \div a\)). We then repeat. The following shows an example
    🔗
    Example 6.4.11.
    Find the GCF using Euclid’s Algorithm Find \(GCF(144,180)\) using Euclid’s Algorithm:
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, show that \(180 \div 144 = 1 R\;36\) (if you need to do long division on this, go ahead). Therefore,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}GCF(180,144) = GCF(144,36)\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Next, we will repeat and do \(144 \div 36\text{,}\) which is \(4\) and no remainder. This shows that
    \begin{equation*} GCF(144,36) = GCF(36,0) \end{equation*}
    and the greatest common factor between 36 and 0 is 36. This shows that \(GCF(180,144) = 36\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s look at another example.
    🔗
    Example 6.4.12.
    Find \(GCF(3315,135)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First find \(3315 \div 135\) using long division:
    🔗
    3315135
    🔗
    Since \(3315 \div 135=24 R75\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} GCF(3315,135)\amp =GCF(135,75) \qquad \qquad \text{and since $135 \div 75 = 1 R 60$} \\ \amp = GCF(75,60), \qquad \qquad \text{and since $75 \div 60 = 1 R 15$} \\ \amp = GCF(60,15), \qquad \qquad\text{and since $60 \div 15 = 4 R 0$} \\ \amp = GCF(15,0) = 15\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This shows that the original problem \(GCF(3315,135)=15\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 6.4.1.2 Relationship between LCM and GCF

    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}LCM(a,b)\amp = \frac{ab}{GCF(a,b)}\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Example 6.4.13.
    First find \(GCF(360,135)\) using Euclid’s Algorithm. Then use the above expression to find \(LCM(360,135)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    360135
    🔗
    Since \(360 \div 135=2 R90\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}GCF(360,135)\amp =GCF(135,90) \quad \text{and since $135 \div 90 = 1 R 45$} \\ \amp = GCF(90,45) = 45\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}LCM(360,135) = \frac{ 360 \cdot 135}{45}= 360 \cdot 3 = 1080\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
