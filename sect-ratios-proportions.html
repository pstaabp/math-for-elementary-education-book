    Section 8.1 Ratios and Proportions

    Objectives

    • Understand a ratio and proportions.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Be able to create diagrams with ratios and proportions.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Be able to solve problems using ratios.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    When we measure things, it is extremely important to use units. Add 3 milk to 2 flours makes no sense in a recipe. Did you mean cups? bags? For a particular recipe the exact amount is crucial, however, we can scale a recipe (double it, half it, for example) easily and if so, the relative amounts are important. We can say that a recipe calls for twice as much flour as sugar and we extend this to the idea of proportional and ratios.
    🔗

    Definition 8.1.1.

    We say that the ratio between two quantities is \(A:B\) if there is a unit so that the first quantity measures \(A\) units and the second measure \(B\) units.
    🔗
    However, in writing the ratio, one does not specify the unit.
    🔗
    🔗
    We will often compare two things in the same unit and then the ratio is the numbers (without the units) of each.
    🔗

    Example 8.1.2.

    • A recipe calls for 2 cups of flour and 1 cup of sugar. The ratio of flour to sugar is \(2:1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If a recipe calls for 3 cups of milk and 2 cups of flour, then the ratio of milk to flour is \(3:2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We can build a ratio for any pair of quantities. Consider the following example.
    🔗

    Example 8.1.3.

    Bernard has 3 marbles for every 2 that Zara has. This means that the ratio of Bernard’s marbles to Zara’s is 3:2.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.1.1 Extending Ratios

    We can also extend ratios to more than two quantities.
    🔗

    Example 8.1.4.

    Art, Beth and Cheryl each has some money in their pockets. Art has 20, Beth has 10 and Cheryl has $40.
    🔗
    The ratio of the amount of money that Art has to Beth to Cheryl is 20:10:40.
    🔗
    🔗
    Astute readers will note that the above ratio is not the simplest and we will address this next.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.1.2 Equivalent Ratios

    Definition 8.1.5.

    Two ratios are equivalent (are “equal ratios” ) if one obtained from the other by multiplying or dividing all of the measurements by the same nonzero number. That is, the ratio \(a:b\) is equivalent to \((na):(nb)\) for all positive whole numbers \(a, b\) and \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.1.6.

    The ratios 3:2 and 6:4 are equivalent because multiplying both 3 and 2 by 2 gives the second ratio.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.1.3 Word Problems

    This section, more than others, depends on word problems due to the nature of the way that ratios are used. That is, they tend to be used in applications rather than in more pure mathematical sense.
    🔗

    Example 8.1.7.

    Mary and her brother shared $28 in a 3:4 ratio. How much money did Mary have?
    🔗
    Solution.
    We first start with a diagram
    🔗
    This type (and most ratio problems) naturally fit the unitary method. We start with the 7 units and try to get to 3 units.
    \begin{align*} 7\text{ units}\amp= \$28 \qquad \div 7 \\ 1\text{ unit}\amp= \$4 \qquad \times 3 \\ 3\text{ units}\amp= \$12. \end{align*}
    🔗
    This shows that Mary has 3 units. Therefore, Mary has $12.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The next word problem shows an extended ratio.
    🔗

    Example 8.1.8.

    John, Tom and David share a sum of money in the ratio 4:5:6. If David receives $60 more than John, find the sum of money shared by the three boys.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Again, start with a diagram. There are three amounts so we’ll make a stacked bar diagram. Since the ratio is given, we’ll make the bar lengths in this ratio.
    🔗
    Again, we’ll use the unitary method and start with 2 units, because we know that is $60. The total number of units is \(4+5+6=15\text{,}\) so we try to get to 15 units.
    \begin{align*} 2\text{ units}\amp= \$60 \qquad \div 2 \\ 1\text{ unit}\amp= \$30 \qquad \times 15\\ 15\text{ units}\amp= \$450. \end{align*}
    🔗
    The sum of money shared by all three boys is $450.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.1.4 Proportions

    A proportion is a statement that two ratios are equal.
    🔗

    Example 8.1.9.

    In order to make play dough, the following table is used to determine the amount of flour and salt to use:
    🔗
    cups of Flour 2 4 8 10
    cups of Salt 1 2 4 5
    The ratio of flour to salt is 2:1 or 4:2 or 8:4. This discusses the proportion of flour to salt.
    🔗
    🔗
    We will often use proportions (or equivalent ratios) to solve problems.
    🔗

    Example 8.1.10.

    The two unequal sides of a rectangle has a ratio of 2:3. If the shorter side is 10 cm, find the longer side.
    🔗
    Solution.
    In this case, we use the equivalent ratios to solve this problem with \(\ell\) as the length of the longer side.
    \begin{equation*} 2:3= 10:\ell \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We note that since \(10=2\times 5\text{,}\) then \(\ell = 3 \times 5 = 15\text{.}\) The length of the longer side is 15.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.1.11.

    The ratio of blue marbles to green marbles in a jar is 2:5. If there are 24 blue marbles, how many marbles are there altogether?
    🔗
    Solution.
    In this case, we’ll solve this first using proportions, then as bar diagrams.
    🔗
    Using proportions
    🔗
    In this case the number of blue marbles to green marbles is proportional to 2:5 or we can say that
    \begin{equation*} 2:5 = 24: G \end{equation*}
    where \(G\) is the number of green marbles. Since these are equivalent, multiplying 2 by 12 gives 24, therefore multiplying 5 by 12 gives \(G\) so \(G=5 \cdot 12=60\text{.}\) The total number of marbles is \(60+24=84\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Using a bar diagram
    🔗
    Again, start with a diagram. We’ll make it stacked with the two colors and the length according to the proportion.
    🔗
    We’ll use the unitary method start with 2 units being 24 and get to \(2+5=7\) units.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} 2\text{ units}\amp= 24 \qquad \div 2 \\ 1\text{ units}\amp= 12 \qquad \times 7\\ 7\text{ units}\amp= 84 \end{align*}
    🔗
    There are 84 marbles.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.1.5 Changing Ratios

    Another common problem with ratios is that of changing ratios. There is some situation where the ratio is given and items are added or removed and we want to know the new ratio. This is solved in the same way as before, but often a second diagram is needed.
    🔗

    Example 8.1.12. Changing Ratios.

    48 marbles are in a bag with a ratio of 2:1 white to red marbles. If 4 more white marbles are added, what is the new ratio of white to red marbles? Write the ratio in reduced form.
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, we’ll do a diagram with the situation before marbles are added. We’ll need to know the number of white and red marbles.
    🔗
    We’ll use the unitary method with 3 units equalling 48.
    \begin{align*} 3 \text{ units}\amp= 48 \qquad \div 3 \\ 1\text{ unit}\amp= 16 \qquad \times 2\\ 2\text{ units}\amp= 32 \end{align*}
    🔗
    And this shows that there are 16 red marbles and 32 white marbles.
    🔗
    If 4 more white marbles are added then the ratio of white to red marbles is 36:16. To reduce this note that
    \begin{equation*} 36:16= (4 \times 9): (4 \times 4) =9:4 \end{equation*}
    so the new ratio of white to red is 9:4.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
