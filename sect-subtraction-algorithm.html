    Section 5.2 Subtraction Algorithms

    Objectives

    • Understanding subtraction for larger numbers with place value.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use a chip model to model subtraction problems with the take-away interpretation.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Develop an understanding of the column algorithm from the chip model.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Perform subtraction in bases other than 10 using the chip model and column algorithm.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Since subtraction is finding the missing addend in a summation problem, it is not surprising that the subtraction algorithm is similar to the addition algorithm. Let’s see an example with two 3-digit numbers.
    🔗
    Consider the subtraction algorithm \(578-253\text{.}\) We can write this in expand form as
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 578 - 253\amp= (500 + 70 + 8) - (200 + 50 + 3) \\ \amp= (500-200) + (70 - 50) + (8 -3 ) \\ \amp = 300 + 20 + 5 = 325. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    or can be viewed in column format as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{cccc} \amp 5 \amp 7 \amp 8 \\ - \amp 2 \amp 5 \amp 3 \\ \hline \amp 3 \amp 2 \amp 5 \\ \end{array} \end{equation*}
    where subtraction is done in each column. Also, note that the top number in each column (place value) is greater than the bottom number in that column, so we can subtract without any issues.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.1 Coin Model

    Recall that a coin model for this problem is using the take-away interpretation. Practically, this means that put the minuend down in coins and cross out the subtrahend. This could look like:
    🔗
    And the chips that are left are 3 hundreds, 2 tens and 5 ones or 325.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.2 Chip Model for Subtraction

    We now apply the chip model for subtraction. We will put the minuend as coins in a table labeled with the chip value in each column. This will look like:
    🔗
    And then subtracting the subtrahend or 253 would be to cross out 2 hundreds chips, 5 tens chips and 3 ones chips. Because there is enough of each of those, this can be done as
    🔗
    The result from this is identical to the coin model or 3 hundreds, 2 tens and 5 ones or 325.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.3 Subtraction with Unbundling

    The next important step is to perform a subtraction problem with unbundling. We’ll see it first in a chip model for \(53-28\text{.}\) Let’s first examine this in expand form:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 53 - 28 \amp = (50+3)- (20+8) \\ \amp = (50-20) + (3-8) \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    and the problem with this is that at grades for doing subtraction \(3-8\) doesn’t make sense. We can’t take 8 away from 3. So the 50 needs to be written as \(40+10\) and then write the 10 with the ones as:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \phantom{53-28} \amp= (40 -20) + (10+3-8) \\ \amp= 20 + 5 =25 \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    What we have done is unbundled a ten to make 10 ones. We can unbundle any denomination more than ones to make 10 smaller denominators. For example, we can unbundle a 1000 as 10 hundreds.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.4 Chip Model of Subtraction with Unbundling

    We now turn to the chip model for the problem \(53-28\text{.}\) Again, we will start with writing the chips for the minuend \(53\) or
    🔗
    and our goal is to remove 2 tens and 8 ones, however, there are only 3 ones to remove in the ones column. We take one of the chips in the tens column and turn it into 10 ones chips like:
    🔗
    and notice that we have removed a ten (by crossing it out) and putting down ten chips in the ones column (red) and clearly show how the chip was unbundled.
    🔗
    The last step is to cross out the subtrahend or 2 tens and 8 ones.
    🔗
    and the diagram above shows that the result is 2 tens and 5 ones or 25.
    🔗

    Remark 5.2.1. Tips for creating good chip models for subtraction.

    Here’s a few things that make a clear subtraction chip model with take-away interpretation and are recommended:
    🔗
    • Only put the minuend on the chart with the chips.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Recall that you are only going to cross out the subtrahend. Don’t put the subtrahend on the diagram/chart.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Make sure that you have enough chips to cross out. If you don’t have enough for a particular place value (1s, 10s, 100s, etc.), unbundle a chip from a higher place value, placing 10 chips in the slot you need them in.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Cross out the subtrahend from the diagram.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Count the result. Note the difference should be clear on the diagram from what is not crossed out.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The chip model for \(53-28\) can now be used to show the subtraction algorithm in column format as
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccc} \amp 5 \amp 3 \\ - \amp 2 \amp 8 \\ \hline \end{array} \end{equation*}
    and note that in the ones column the 3 is less that 8, so we need to unbundle a ten from the tens column and add it to the 3 in the ones column to get 13. This is shown as follows:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccc} \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 4} \amp \color {magenta}{\scriptstyle 13} \\ \amp \cancel{5} \amp \cancel{3} \\ - \amp 2 \amp 8 \\ \hline \end{array} \end{equation*}
    And now there are 13 in the ones column and 4 in the tens column, so we can subtract 8 from 13 and 2 from 4 to get the answer of 25.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccc} \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 4} \amp \color {magenta}{\scriptstyle 13} \\ \amp \cancel{5} \amp \cancel{3} \\ - \amp 2 \amp 8 \\ \hline \amp 2 \amp 5 \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The chip models in this section were designed to teach how to make the model. You can do all of the necessary steps on a single diagram, that is, the last diagram in the steps clearly shows the steps.
    🔗
    The next example shows a challenging subtraction problem using chip models.
    🔗

    Example 5.2.2.

    Use a chip model to demonstrate \(3019-2852\) using a chip model.
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, start with the minuend, \(3019\) on a chart:
    🔗
    and notice that we won’t have enough hundreds or tens to cross out, so first unbundle 1 thousand into 10 hundreds and then 1 hundred into 10 tens:
    🔗
    At this point, there is enough chips to cross out (take-away) from the subtrahend. The last step is to do this. This crosses out 2 thousands chips, 8 hundreds chips, 5 tens chips and 2 ones chips or
    🔗
    And the difference (result) is the chips remaining. This shows 167.
    🔗
    Let’s now perform the column algorithm for \(3019-2852\text{.}\) Start with the problem in column format:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccccc} \amp 3 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 9 \\ - \amp 2 \amp 8 \amp 5 \amp 2 \\ \hline \end{array} \end{equation*}
    and now we subtract from right to left. The ones column has the larger number on top, so we can just subtract:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccccc} \amp 3 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 9 \\ - \amp 2 \amp 8 \amp 5 \amp 2 \\ \hline \amp \amp \amp \amp 7 \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Now for the tens column, since 1 is less than 5, we need to unbundle a hundred from the hundreds column. However, this is not possible, so instead, we need to unbundle a thousand from the thousands column and then unbundle a hundred from the hundreds column. This is shown as follows:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccccc} \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 2} \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 10} \amp \amp \\ \amp \cancel{3} \amp \cancel{0} \amp 1 \amp 9 \\ - \amp 2 \amp 8 \amp 5 \amp 2 \\ \hline \amp \amp \amp \amp 7 \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Now we can unbundle one of the 100s and add to the tens column as follows:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccccc} \amp \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 9} \amp \amp \\ \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 2} \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle \cancel{10}} \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 11} \amp \\ \amp \cancel{3} \amp \cancel{0} \amp 1 \amp 9 \\ - \amp 2 \amp 8 \amp 5 \amp 2 \\ \hline \amp \amp \amp \amp 7 \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Now each of the columns have the top number larger than the bottom and we can subtract column by column.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccccc} \amp \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 9} \amp \amp \\ \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 2} \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle \cancel{10}} \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 11} \amp \\ \amp \cancel{3} \amp \cancel{0} \amp \cancel{1} \amp 9 \\ - \amp 2 \amp 8 \amp 5 \amp 2 \\ \hline \amp \amp 1 \amp 6 \amp 7 \\ \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Note that the column algorithm is identical to the chip model, but the column algorithm is more efficient than the chip model.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    As discussed, there are many reasons for knowing the algorithms for addition and subtraction. The following example shows that we need to think a bit more abstractly to find the solution (and not let the addition and subtraction get in the way).
    🔗

    Example 5.2.3.

    Produce a Teacher’s Solution to the following word problem: One week, a candy company produced a total of 5327 pieces of candy (red, blue and yellow). If 2378 pieces are red and 1263 are blue, how many were yellow?
    🔗
    Solution.
    This is a two-step word problem that we can do as a single diagram as the following:
    🔗
    The first operation is \(1263+2378\) and this would most like be given in a course in which the column algorithm is taught, so we will use the column algorithm to find this sum.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccccc} \amp \amp {\color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 1}} \amp {\color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 1}} \amp \\ \amp 1 \amp 2 \amp 6 \amp 3 \\ + \amp 2 \amp 3 \amp 7 \amp 8 \\ \hline \amp 3 \amp 6 \amp 4 \amp 1 \\ \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For the next step subtract the result 3641 from 5327 and again we’ll use the column algorithm:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccccc} \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 4} \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 12} \amp \amp \\ \amp \amp \color{magenta}{\scriptstyle \cancel{2}} \amp {\color{magenta}{\scriptstyle 12}} \amp \\ \amp \cancel{5} \amp \cancel{3} \amp \cancel{2} \amp 7 \\ - \amp 3 \amp 6 \amp 4 \amp 1 \\ \hline \amp 1 \amp 6 \amp 8 \amp 6 \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    There were 1686 pieces of yellow candy made in that week.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Note that we didn’t use the chip model in the Teacher’s solution. The chip model is helpful for understanding the column algorithms for subtraction and addition, but is not needed once learned well.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.2.5 Base-5 Subtraction

    We continue now with base-5 arithmetic. Recall that the important takeaway from this section is a deeper understanding of subtraction and the subtraction algorithm, so we will use the chip model to understand subtraction in base-5.
    🔗
    We will first use a coin model to understand base-5 subtraction. This works exactly like decimal subtraction we just saw.
    🔗
    Let’s try \(3024_{\text{5}}-1341_{\text{5}}\text{.}\) Recall that do use the chip model we only put the subtrahend on the diagram at first and remember that the headers are powers of 5.
    🔗
    Since we wish to cross on one 125s, three 25s, two 5s and 1 one, but there aren’t enough 25s and 5s, so we unbundle one each of these, remember that each chip’s value is 5 of the smaller chip.
    🔗
    Now for the last step, we cross out the subtrahend:
    🔗
    The result is 1 fiftoon, 1 quarter, 3 nickels and 3 pennies or \(1133_{\text{5}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We can also do this with the column algorithm:
    🔗
    Start with the subtrahend and minuend stacked:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}\begin{array}{ccccc}\amp3\amp0\amp2\amp4 \\ -\amp1\amp3\amp4\amp1 \\ \hline\end{array}\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We can subtract the rightmost column:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}\begin{array}{ccccc}\amp3\amp0\amp2\amp4 \\ -\amp1\amp3\amp4\amp1 \\ \hline \amp\amp\amp\amp3\end{array}\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The 2nd rightmost column needs more so rebundle, except we can’t, so to the leftmost column:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}\begin{array}{ccccc}\amp {\scriptstyle 2} \amp{\scriptstyle 10}\amp\amp \\ \amp\cancel{3}\amp\cancel{0}\amp2\amp4 \\ -\amp1\amp3\amp4\amp1 \\ \hline \amp\amp\amp\amp3\end{array}\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    where \(10\) means \(10_{\text{5}}\) or 5 in decimal. We can now rebundle from the 2nd column to the 3rd:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}\begin{array}{ccccc}\amp \amp {\scriptstyle 4}\amp \\ \amp {\scriptstyle 2} \amp{\cancel{\scriptstyle 10}}\amp{\scriptstyle 12}\amp \\ \amp\cancel{3}\amp\cancel{0}\amp\cancel{2}\amp4 \\ -\amp1\amp3\amp4\amp1 \\ \hline \amp\amp\amp\amp3\end{array}\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and once again \(12\) means \(12_{\text{5}}\) or 1 five and 2 ones or 7 in decimal. Now we can subtract in each column:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}\begin{array}{ccccc}\amp \amp {\scriptstyle 4}\amp \\ \amp {\scriptstyle 2} \amp{\cancel{\scriptstyle 10}}\amp{\scriptstyle 12}\amp \\ \amp\cancel{3}\amp\cancel{0}\amp\cancel{2}\amp4 \\ -\amp1\amp3\amp4\amp1 \\ \hline \amp1\amp1\amp3\amp3\end{array}\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    and the result is \(1133_{\text{5}}\text{,}\) just like we saw with the chip model.
    🔗
    🔗
