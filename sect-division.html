    Section 2.5 Division

    Division of whole numbers is defined in terms of multiplication.
    🔗
    The number \(54 \div 6\) is defined as the number missing in the multiplication \(6 \times \boxed{\phantom{X}}= 54\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{54}_{\text{dividend}}\div \underbrace{9}_{\text{divisor}} = \underbrace{6}_{\text{quotient}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    There are two distinct ways of thinking about division because of the commutative properties of multiplication. The division problem \(18 \div 3\) can be thought of as either
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 18\amp= 3 \times \boxed{\phantom{x}}\amp\amp\text{or}\amp18\amp= \boxed{\phantom{x}}\times 3\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The answer to either of these is the same, 6, but can be interpreted a bit differently.
    🔗
    • The first is “18 is three groups of what size?” (partitive division)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The second is “18 is how many groups of size 3” (measurement division)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.3 Division with Remainders

    What if we need to answer the problem: How many dozens of eggs are in 30 eggs?
    🔗
    We know that two dozen is 24 eggs and three dozen is 36 eggs, so 30 eggs is two dozen plus some left over.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 30 = (12 \times \underbrace{\boxed{\phantom{x}}}_{\text{quotient}}) + \underbrace{\boxed{\phantom{x}}}_{\text{remainder}} \end{equation*}
    and in this case because \(30 = 12 \times 2 + 6\text{,}\) the quotient is 2 and the remainder is 6.
    🔗
    Models with division and remainders are quite helpful. Below, we show 3 eggs and 2 groups of 12 (dashed) and the remainder of 6.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.5.3.

    Quotient-Remainder Theorem For any two whole numbers \(A\) and \(k\) with \(k\neq 0\text{,}\) there are unique whole numbers \(q\) (the quotient) and \(r\) (the remainder) such that
    \begin{equation*} A = (k \times q) + r \end{equation*}
    and \(0 \leq r \lt k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    This can be demonstrated in a general way with the following rectangular model.
    🔗
    where the \(\cdots\) throughout the diagram indicates that there are an indeterminate number of boxes. Note that the number of rows is the divisor and that the remainder \(r\) is the number of boxes that don’t fit into a complete row.
    🔗
    This is a visual way to see the quotient-remainder theorem, especially the statements that \(0 \leq r \lt k\text{,}\) that is the number of extra boxes (the remainder) can be 0 and up to (but not including) the number of rows.

    Subsection 2.5.4 Division by Zero

    What is \(6 \div 0\text{?}\) Using the definition of division, it is the missing factor. That is, we can write this as
    \begin{equation*} 6= 0 \times \boxed{\phantom{x}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since nothing can go in the box to make this equation work, the answer to this is undefined.
    🔗
    We can also think of this in the division interpretation. From a partitive interpretation, \(6 \div 0\) represents the size of the group if 6 is split into 0 groups. Alternatively, from a measurement interpretation, the result is the number of groups of size 0. In either case, these don’t make sense.
    🔗
    What about \(0 \div 0\text{?}\) Write this as
    \begin{equation*} 0 = 0 \times \boxed{\phantom{x}} \end{equation*}
    and now in this case, any number works, but we still say this is undefined.
    🔗
