    Section 4.3 Estimation and Rounding

    Objectives

    • Be able to round to nearest power of ten.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use rounding to quickly perform estimations of numerical expressions.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use range estimates to know where the actual answer lies as well as use range estimates to solve word problems.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    This section is all about getting approximations to numbers. It is often the case that we need to know “about” what the answer is, not exactly the answer.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.3.1 Rounding

    An important skill will be to find a number to the nearest multiple of 10, 100, 1000, etc. For small numbers this is relatively straightforward, but for larger numbers a grasp of place value is key.
    🔗

    Example 4.3.1.

    Round 73 to the nearest ten.
    🔗
    Solution.
    First note that 73 could either be rounded up to 80 or down to 70. Because 73 is closer to 70, then this is the answer.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The following is the rounding algorithm that is typically used.
    🔗

    Note 4.3.2. Rounding Algorithm.

    • To round a number to the nearest ten, if the ones digit is 0,1,2,3,4 round down, and 5,6,7,8,9 round up.
      🔗
      🔗
    • To round a number to the nearest hundred, if the tens digit is 0,1,2,3,4 round down, if the tens digit is 5,6,7,8,9 round up.
      🔗
      🔗
    • To round a number to the nearest thousand, if the hundreds digit is 0,1,2,3,4 round down, if the hundreds digit is 5,6,7,8,9 round up.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Rounding to higher denominations is similar.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    This can be understood visually with a number line. Consider the problem of rounding 73 to the nearest ten. We’ll draw the number line with 73 with the two options 70 and 80.
    🔗
    The two options 70 and 80 are 3 units and 7 units away from 73 as shown with:
    🔗
    If a number’s ones digit is 5 and you are asked to round to the nearest ten, then this might be a bit of a puzzle. Consider rounding 55 to the nearest ten. If we plot on the number line similar to above:
    🔗
    This is clear that 55 is equally close to 50 or 60. The convention is that if the rounding digit is a 5 that we round up.
    🔗

    Rounding Decimals.

    Rounding is also important with decimals and we will see how to do this in Chapter 10.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.3.2 Estimation

    Estimation is the process of quickly finding an operation that is close but not necessary the exact answer.
    🔗
    Why is this a good skill to learn?
    🔗
    • Helps with learning place value.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Reinforces mental math.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Determines if answers are probably correct.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Helps in long division.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Rounding.

    Rounding By rounding numbers to the nearest ten (or power of ten), we can get a good idea of the size of a number.
    🔗
    Example 4.3.3.
    Find \(38 \times 19\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since \(38 \approx 40\) and \(19 \approx 20\text{,}\) \(38 \times 19\approx 40 \times 20 =800\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We now look at some important steps for students to work through in estimation:
    🔗

    Step 1:.

    Rounding to 1-digit arithmetic skills:
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle 768+121 \approx 800+100=900\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(1589-425 \approx 1600-400=1200\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(31.5 \times 7.2 \approx 30 \times 7 = 210\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(476 \div 6 \approx 480 \div 6 = 80\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Step 2:.

    Keep track of place value.
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle 8105 \times 714 \approx 8000 \times 700 = 56 \times 1000 \times 100 = 5,600,000\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle 71034 \div 910 \approx 72000 \div 900 = 720 \div 9 = 80.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.3.3 What is a good estimation?

    Is all estimation good? For example, if we try to find \(17 \times 65\text{.}\) Here are some possible answers:
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle 17 \times 65 \approx 20 \times 60 = 1200\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle 17 \times 65 \approx 20 \times 70 = 1400\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle 17 \times 65 \approx 15 \times 70 = 1050\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle 17 \times 65 \approx 15 \times 60 = 900\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Although any one if these is a reasonable estimate, The second one rounded both factors up and the resulting approximation is larger than the actual answer. The fourth rounded both factors down and the resulting approximation is smaller than the actual answer.
    🔗
    Perhaps if you’re trying to get close, use one that rounds one up and one down, so either the first or third.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.3.4 Range Estimates

    As we saw above, what we mean by a good estimate depends on what we want. However, it’s common to want to have both a high and low estimate so we know where our answer lies. For example as shown above, \(17 \times 65\) is between 900 and 1400. A range estimate is a pair of estimates: one too high, and one too low.
    🔗
    Finding range estimates depends on the operation. We saw in the example above, for both addition and multiplication, a high estimate rounds both factors up and a low one rounds both down. However, for subtraction and division, a high estimate rounds the subtrahend and dividend up and then the minuend or divisor down. A low estimate reverses these. We look at end of these with examples.
    🔗

    Range Estimates for Addition.

    Example 4.3.4.
    Find a range estimate for \(478 + 931\) to the nearest 50.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For addition a high estimate will round both up to the nearest 50
    \begin{equation*} 478 + 931 \approx 500 + 950 = 1450 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and we have used compensation mental math to do the last calculation. A low estimate is
    \begin{equation*} 478 + 931 \approx 450 + 900 = 1350 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This allows us to know that the actual value is between 1350 and 1450.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Range Estimates for Subtraction.

    For subtraction,
    🔗
    Example 4.3.5.
    Give a range estimate for \(318 - 184\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    As mentioned above, because a larger minuend makes for a smaller difference, to find the high and low estimates requires mixing the rounding.
    🔗
    High Estimate
    🔗
    For this a simple estimate is to round \(318\) up to \(400\) and then to round the minuend \(184\) down to \(100\text{.}\) This would result in
    \begin{equation*} 318 - 184 \approx 400 - 100 = 300. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    But this is not a good estimate. Let’s instead round to the nearest ten or
    \begin{equation*} 318 - 184 \approx 320 - 180 = 140 \end{equation*}
    where \(320 -180\) can be found easily with compensation as \(320-180 = 340 -200 = 140\)
    🔗
    Low Estimate
    🔗
    If we round to the nearest 100, then we will round down the subtrahend and up the minuend or
    \begin{equation*} 318 - 184 \approx 300 - 200 = 100 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This isn’t a great estimate. Let’s try rounding to the nearest tens or
    \begin{equation*} 318 - 184 \approx 310 - 190 = 120 \end{equation*}
    where mentally you can do \(310 - 190 = 320 - 200 = 120\) with compensation.
    🔗
    🔗
    Therefore the best estimate puts the range as between 120 and 140, which is a good tight estimate.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Range Estimates for Multiplication.

    Example 4.3.6.
    Find a range estimate of \(18 \times 315\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    For this, we will find both a high and low estimate of both numbers. We’ll round 18 to the nearest 10 and 315 to the nearest hundred.
    Low Estimate
    🔗
    \(\displaystyle 18 \times 315 \approx 10 \times 300 = 3000. \)
    🔗
    High Estimate
    🔗
    \(\displaystyle 18 \times 315 \approx 20 \times 400 = 8000\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Because both numbers in the low estimate are smaller than the original, the actual value of \(18 \times 315\) must be larger than 3000. Similarly, since both numbers in the high estimate is larger than the original, the actual value is smaller than 8000. We can say then that \(18 \times 315\) is between 3000 and 8000.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Looking at the last estimate, you may think that the range 3000 to 8000 is very wide. However, if you are using it to check an answer it could be useful to eliminate errors often associated with place value.
    🔗
    However, what if we want a tighter range estimate. We can round to other values instead. For example, for a low estimate we can round 18 to 15 and 315 to 300 to get
    \begin{equation*} 18 \times 315 \approx 15 \times 300 = 4500 \end{equation*}
    where \(15 \times 3\) is not too difficult to do mentally. If you’re better at mental math another low estimate could be
    \begin{equation*} 18 \times 315 \approx 18 \times 300 = 5400 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    On the high estimate, it’s natural to round the 18 to 20, but if we round 315 to 320 instead we get
    \begin{equation*} 18 \times 315 \approx 20 \times 320 = 6400 \end{equation*}
    or if you refine those mental math skills
    \begin{equation*} 18 \times 315 \approx 20 \times 315 = 6300 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus, a tighter range estimate for this problem is 5400 to 6300.
    🔗
    🔗

    Range Estimates for Division.

    Similar to subtraction, a range estimate for division requires different rounding for the dividend and divisor. For a high estimate, round the dividend up and the divisor down. For a low estimate, round the dividend down and the divisor up.
    🔗
    Also, since division either often results in a remainder or as we will see a fraction, estimates with division often require either rounding to compatible numbers, that is ones that either result in a whole number or if there is a remainder, round the remainder up or down.
    🔗
    Example 4.3.7.
    Find a range estimate for \(378 \div 14\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    In this case, a dividend in the hundreds and the divisor in the tens will probably result in a quotient in the tens. Thus, we should round the dividend to the nearest tens instead of hundreds.
    🔗
    High Estimate
    🔗
    For a high estimate, we need to round the dividend up and the divisor down.
    \begin{equation*} 378 \div 14 \approx 380 \div 10 = 38 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Low Estimate
    🔗
    For a low estimate, we need to round the dividend down and the divisor up.
    \begin{equation*} 378 \div 14 \approx 360 \div 20 = 18. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Thus a range estimate for this is 18 to 38.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.3.5 Teaching Estimates

    Teaching estimates requires that we teach students why to estimate as well as what makes a good estimate. Often, we can use problems with clear results to emphasize this. As with other parts of mathematics, good practical examples are often helpful. We look at a few examples.
    🔗

    Example 4.3.8.

    Johnny and Felix both collect Magic, the Gathering cards. Johnny has 3 piles and knows that there are 51, 69 and 73 in the piles. Felix has 4 piles of cards and there are 29, 52, 43 and 35 cards in the piles. Who has more cards?
    🔗
    Solution.
    As before, we could add up the number for each, but perhaps an estimate is sufficient.
    🔗
    We will find a range estimate for both of them.
    Johnny
    🔗
    The low estimate could be
    \begin{equation*} 51 + 69 + 73 \approx 50 + 60 + 70 = 180 \end{equation*}
    and a high estimate would be
    \begin{equation*} 51 + 69 + 73 \approx 60 + 70 + 80 = 210 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Felix
    🔗
    The low estimate could be
    \begin{equation*} 29 + 52+42+35 \approx 20 + 50 + 40 + 30 = 140 \end{equation*}
    and a high estimate could be
    \begin{equation*} 29 + 52+42+35 \approx 30 + 60 + 50 + 40 = 180 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    We see that Johnny has a least 180 cards and Felix has at most. If we look at the estimate, there was rounding going on, to this means that Johnny has more cards.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.3.9.

    There are 24 bottles of apple juice in a case. Each apple juice is 12 ounces. A school needs 1000 ounces of juice for an event. How many cases would they need?
    🔗
    Solution.
    If we estimate the ounces in a case, then
    \begin{equation*} 24 \times 12 \approx 24 \times 10 = 240 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    And then to determine the number of cases, we would need to estimate \(1000 \div 240\)
    🔗
    And this is a little more that 4. Would the school need to then purchase 5 cases?
    🔗
    Clearly to be safe that would be wise, however, if the number of ounces in a case is more than 250, then the school would only need 4 cases.
    🔗
    Notice that by rounding 12 down to 10, we have underestimated by an amount that is \(2 \times 24\) or more than 10. Thus we know that \(24 \times 12 \) is greater than 250. Therefore 4 cases is plenty.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The next problem helps with the long division algorithm in Chapter 5.
    🔗

    Example 4.3.10.

    Is \(7891 \div 13\) closest to 610, 61 or 6.1?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since we are just looking for closest, we can do just an estimate.
    \begin{equation*} 7891 \div 13 \approx 7000 \div 10 = 700 \end{equation*}
    and thus 610 is the best answer.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.3.11.

    Claire has $20 to buy groceries. If she buys products that cost $1.99, $1.25, $4.95, $8.15 and $0.95, does she have enough? What if she also has to pay 5% sales tax.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Even though we haven’t covered decimals yet, you the student have and should have the ability to understand this problem.
    🔗
    Since we want to make sure she has enough, if we produce a high estimate, and it comes in below $20, she will have enough.
    \begin{equation*} 1.99+ 1.25 + 4.95 + 8.15 + 0.95 \approx 2 + 2 + 5 + 9 + 1 = 19 \end{equation*}
    So it appears she will have enough.
    🔗
    To include the 5% sales tax, note that for $20, 5% is $1, so that would add another dollar to the total. Again, since this is a high estimate, Claire will have enough money.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
