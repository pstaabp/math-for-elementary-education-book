    Section 7.2 Addition and Subtraction of Fractions

    Since clearly that fractions are numbers, we seek to perform the same operations that we did with whole numbers. We first start with addition and subtraction of fractions. This is done is two stages. The first stage shows and explains how to add and subtract with the same denominators and then it is moved onto the more-difficult addition and subtraction with unlike denominators.
    🔗

    Subsection 7.2.1 Addition and Subtracting with same denominators

    We move on to introducing addition and subtraction. We move from understanding that
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle 2 \text{~cookies}+ 5 \text{~cookies}= 7 \text{~cookies}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle 2 \text{~feet}+ 5 \text{~feet}= 7 \text{~feet}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    as long as we are talking about keeping the unit consistent that
    • \(\displaystyle 2 \text{~ninths}+ 5 \text{~ninths}= 7 \text{~ninths}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    and that adding and subtracting fractions in a common unit is identical to whole number addition and subtraction with units. That is,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2}{9}+ \frac{5}{9}= \frac{7}{9} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This leads to the following fraction rule with the same denominator.
    🔗

    Property 7.2.1. Addition and Subtraction of Fractions with Like Denominators.

    Let \(a,b\) and \(c\) be positive whole numbers. Then
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \frac{a}{b}+ \frac{c}{b} \amp = \frac{a+c}{b},\amp\frac{a}{b}- \frac{c}{b}\amp= \frac{a-c}{b}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Models for Addition and Subtraction.

    Of course, when done correctly, models are important. For example, look at the model for \(\frac{2}{4}+ \frac{1}{4}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    which clearly shows that the result is \(\dfrac{3}{4}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.2.2.

    Bernie had a candy bar and gave his sister Flo \(\frac{1}{5}\) of it to eat. He ate \(\frac{2}{5}\) of it. How much was left?

    Solution.
    We start with a diagram for this.
    🔗
    The amount that Bernie and Flo ate is 3 of the pieces with shows that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2}{5}+ \frac{1}{5}= \frac{3}{5} \end{equation*}
    of the candy bar. The remaining part is two pieces or \(\frac{2}{5}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So there is \(\frac{2}{5}\) of the candy bar left.
    🔗
    🔗

    Property 7.2.4. Fraction-Division Equivalence.
    Let \(a\) and \(b\) be positive whole numbers, then.
    \begin{equation*} a \div b = \frac{a}{b}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This rule shows that division is the same as a fraction and this allows students to be able to solve fraction problems via division and division problems via ideas from fractions.

    Example 7.2.5.
    If 5 people equally share a 32 oz. soda, how much does each get?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Knowing this is a division problem \(32 \div 5\) and there are four possible answers to this:
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle 6 R2\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \frac{32}{5}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle 6\frac{2}{5}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle 6.4\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Which is the correct (or best) way to write the answer. The first is probably incorrect, because that would mean removing 2 ounces from the soda and then splitting the remaining 30 oz. This doesn’t make too much sense, because we can divide 2 ounces of soda equally.
    🔗
    The 2nd answer, although more correct, is difficult to think of. How do you measure \(32/5\) ounces?
    🔗
    The 3rd and fourth are good solutions to this. Each would depend on how to measure the ounces. Some measuring cup may list fractions of ounces as fractions and other may list them as decimals.

    Subsection 7.2.4 Algebraic Form for Adding/Subtracting Fractions

    The two examples above can be summarized as the rule for adding/subtraction with unlike denominators. In short we need a common denominator and following shows how to achieve this for the previous two examples:
    🔗

    Example 7.2.8. Addition with Unlike Denominators.

    Use equivalent fractions to find \(\dfrac{1}{3}+\dfrac{2}{5}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Not surprisingly, from the diagrams above, the common denominator is the product of the denominators or in this case \(15\text{.}\) We can write
    \begin{align*} \frac{1}{3}\amp= \frac{1 \cdot 5}{3 \cdot 5}= \frac{5}{15}\amp\frac{2}{5}\amp= \frac{2 \cdot 3}{5 \cdot 3}= \frac{6}{15} \end{align*}
    So we can write:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{3}+ \frac{2}{5}= \frac{5}{15}+ \frac{6}{15}= \frac{11}{15} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.2.9. Subtraction with Unlike Denominators.

    Use equivalent fractions to find \(\dfrac{3}{4}-\dfrac{2}{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Again, we find a common denominator which is just \(4 \times 3 = 12\) and find equivalent fractions with this denominator:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{3}{4}- \frac{2}{3}= \frac{3 \cdot 3}{4 \cdot 3}- \frac{2 \cdot 4}{3 \cdot 4}= \frac{9}{12}-\frac{8}{12}= \frac{1}{12} \end{equation*}
    which is the same that we found with the diagrams above.
    🔗
    🔗
    This can be summarized as the following rule.
    🔗

    Property 7.2.10. Adding and Subtracting Fractions with Unlike Denominators.

    Let \(a,b,c\) and \(d\) be positive whole numbers, then
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \frac{a}{b}+ \frac{c}{d}\amp= \frac{ad}{bd}+ \frac{bc}{bd}= \frac{ad+bc}{bd}\amp\frac{a}{b}- \frac{c}{d}\amp= \frac{ad}{bd}- \frac{bc}{bd}= \frac{ad-bc}{bd} \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.2.5 Finding a Common Denominator

    The above examples showed how to find a common denominator by just multiplying the two denominators. In many cases, we can find a simpler example.
    🔗

    Example 7.2.11.

    Find \(\dfrac{5}{18}+ \dfrac{7}{24}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    A straightforward way to do this is to just multiply to find the common denominator as above. In this case,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{5}{18}+ \frac{7}{24}= \frac{5 \cdot 24}{18 \cdot 24}+ \frac{7 \cdot 18}{24 \cdot 18} \end{equation*}
    and this is fine, but notice we probably need to use the algorithms to do \(18 \cdot 24, 7 \cdot 18\) although \(5 \cdot 24=120\) is reasonably done with mental math.
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{120}{432}+ \frac{126}{432}= \frac{246}{432} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This result isn’t in simplest form. You can further reduce the fraction using Equivalent Fractions, but as we will see, often we can to better.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.2.6 Using the Lowest Common Multiple to find a Common Denominator

    Since a common denominator is a multiple of each of the two denominators, it is natural to think of using the least common multiple (LCM) for the common denominator. We’ll do the previous example above first by noting that
    \begin{align*} 18\amp= 2 \cdot 9 = 2 \cdot 3^{2}\amp24\amp= 4 \cdot 6 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 2^{3} \cdot 3. \end{align*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} LCM(18,24) = LCM(2 \cdot 3^{2}, 2^{3} \cdot 3) = 2^{3} \cdot 3^{2} = 8 \cdot 9 = 72. \end{equation*}
    and we repeat the same steps as above except with the common denominator 72 instead of 432.
    🔗
    So we can write
    \begin{equation*} \frac{5}{18}+ \frac{7}{24}= \frac{5 \cdot 4}{18 \cdot 4}+ \frac{7 \cdot 3 }{24 \cdot 3}= \frac{20 + 21}{72}= \frac{41}{72} \end{equation*}
    and this is in simplest form because \(GCF(41,72)=1\) (and 41 is prime). We show another example in which we use subtraction and the LCM.
    🔗

    Example 7.2.12.

    Find \(\dfrac{53}{75}- \dfrac{17}{120}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First note that
    \begin{align*} 75\amp= 3 \cdot 25= 3 \cdot 5^{2}\amp120 = 12 \cdot 10 = (2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3) \cdot (2 \cdot 5) = 2^{3} \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \end{align*}
    and we’ll use this to find the lowest common multiple.
    \begin{align*} LCM(75,120) \amp = LCM(3 \cdot 5^{2}, 2^{3} \cdot 3 \cdot 5) \\ \amp = 2^{3} \cdot 3 \cdot 5^{2} \\ \amp = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 2^{2} \cdot 5^{2} \\ \amp = 6 \cdot 4 \cdot 25 = 600 \end{align*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{53}{75}- \dfrac{17}{120}= \dfrac{53 \cdot 8}{75 \cdot 8}- \dfrac{17 \cdot 5}{120 \cdot 5}= \frac{424 - 85}{600}= \frac{339}{600} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    And although this is correct, it is not in reduced form. Factoring out shows that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{339}{600}= \frac{3 \cdot 113}{3 \cdot 200}= \frac{113}{200} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Some mental math was used to do some of the calculations above. If needed, you can use the algorithm instead to calculate \(53 \cdot 8\) and \(17 \cdot 5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
