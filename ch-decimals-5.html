    Section 10.3 Non-finite Decimals

    Example 10.3.1.

    Write \(\frac{3}{11}\) as a decimal.
    🔗
    Solution.
    311
    🔗
    🔗
    And notice that the 3 and 6 repeat. This is an example of a repeating decimal. We generally write this as
    \begin{equation*} \frac{3}{11} = 0.\overline{36} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 10.3.2.

    Write \(\frac{523}{900}\) as a decimal.
    🔗
    Solution.
    523900
    🔗
    🔗
    and we can write this as
    \begin{equation*} \frac{523}{900}\amp=0.58\overline{1} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 10.3.3.

    The number of digits in the shortest sequence of repeating digits in a repeating decimal is the period.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 10.3.4.

    Write \(\frac{1}{7}\) as a decimal and find its period.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We’ll use long division until we get a repeat.
    🔗
    17
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 10.3.1 Rational-Decimal Fact 2

    Theorem 10.3.5.

    Every rational number can be written as a finite or repeating decimal.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(x=\frac{a}{b}\) be a rational number and without loss of generality, assume that \(a<b\text{.}\) In any step of long division, the quotient-remainder theorem states that the remainder, \(r\) on the step has satisfies \(0 \leq r <b\text{.}\) If \(r=0\text{,}\) then the result is a finite decimal and from Rational-Decimal Fact 1, this only occurs if \(b\) is a product of powers of 2’s and 5’s. In all other cases, \(r\) could be one of \(1,2,3,\ldots, b-1\text{.}\) Thus there are only \(b-1\) choices before the remainder, repeats, therefore the result is a repeating decimal.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 10.3.6.

    The decimal expansion of \(\frac{1}{17}\) is a repeating decimal with period 16.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 10.3.2 Rational-Decimal Fact 3

    Every repeating decimal is a rational number.
    🔗
    We won’t prove this, but will show the algorithm in the two examples below. The proof involves showing that the algorithm works in general.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 10.3.3 Converting Repeating Decimals to rational numbers

    Example 10.3.7.

    Convert \(0.\overline{73}\) to a rational number.
    🔗
    🔗
    Let \(x=0.7373\ldots\) and we will find \(100x\) (because the period is 2) and subtract \(x\)
    \begin{align*} 100 x\amp= 72.7272\ldots \\ -\quad x\amp= 0.727272 \ldots \\ \hline 99 x\amp= 72 \end{align*}
    🔗
    So \(x=\frac{72}{99}\) which can be reduced to \(x=\frac{8}{11}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 10.3.8.

    Convert 0.34555555 to a rational number.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We let \(x=0.34555\ldots\) and notice that the period is 1, so let’s find \(10x-x\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} 10x\amp= 3.4555\ldots \\ x\amp=0.34555\ldots \\ \hline 9x\amp= 3.11 \end{equation*}
    which would result in \(x=\frac{3.11}{9}\text{,}\) which is not a rational number. However, if we multiply the top and bottom by 100, we get \(x=\frac{311}{900}\) which is.
    🔗
    Alternatively, we can write \(1000x-100x\) so that the \(100x\) shifts the repeating part to just right of the decimal point and the other is 10 times this.
    \begin{align*} 1000x\amp= 345.5555\ldots \\ - \qquad 100x\amp= 34.55555 \\ \hline 900 x\amp= 311 \end{align*}
    🔗
    So the solution is \(x= \frac{311}{900}\text{,}\) which is in simplest form. (Why?)
    🔗
    🔗
