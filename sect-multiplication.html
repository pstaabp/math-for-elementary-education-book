    Section 2.4 Multiplication

    Objectives

    • Understand the definition of multiplication and the terms used.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Understand models used in multiplication.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Learn and use the properties of multiplication.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Understand and use the thinking strategies in multiplication.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Multiplication is defined as repeated addition. The result of \(a \times b\) is the second number \(b\) added to itself \(a\) times. For example
    \begin{equation*} 4 \times 6 = \underbrace{6+6+6+6}_{\text{4 times}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    In multiplication, the numbers are called the factors and the result is the product. For example,
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace{4}_{\text{factor}}\times \underbrace{6}_{\text{factor}} = \underbrace{24}_{\text{product}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    • The notation for multiplication in early elementary school is \(\times\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • In higher levels, a dot is used instead, for example \(4\cdot6\text{.}\) This often because algebra is introduced and a common variable \(x\) is used and can be easily confused with \(\times\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.4.2 Properties of Multiplication

    Property 2.4.2.

    The following are the axiomatic properties of multiplication, which holds for all whole numbers \(a,b\) and \(c\text{:}\)
    🔗
    Multiplicative Identity
    🔗
    \(\displaystyle 1 \times a = a\)
    🔗
    Commutative Property
    🔗
    \(\displaystyle a \times b = b \times a\)
    🔗
    Associative Property
    🔗
    \(\displaystyle a \times (b \times c) = (a \times b) \times c\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s look at these properties more in depth.
    🔗
    • Recalling that multiplication is repeated addition, \(1 \times a\) means adding \(a\) one time, so the answer is \(a\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • This makes sense since you can regroup objects. 4 groups of 5 oranges is the same as 5 groups of 4 oranges. A nice way to show this is with a set model. The following shows 4 groups of 5 as:

      🔗
      🔗

    Example 2.4.3.

    What property is each of the following?
    🔗
    (a)
    \(2 \times (6 \times 4) = (2 \times 6) \times 4\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This is the multiplicative associative property.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    \(1 \times 6 = 6\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This is the multiplicative identity.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (c)
    \(3 \times 7 = 7 \times 3\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This is the multiplicative associative property.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Lemma 2.4.4. Any-Order property of Multiplication.

    The operation \(a \times b \times c\) can be done in any order. In fact, the product of any number of factors can be done in any order.
    🔗
    🔗

    Property 2.4.5. Distributive Property of Multiplication.

    If \(a, b\) and \(c\) are whole numbers then
    \begin{equation*} a \times (b + c) = a \times b + a \times c \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Notice that the distributive properties is a blend of addition and multiplication. When you get to algebra, it is used for expanding across parentheses (often with FOILing) or in the reverse it is factoring.
    🔗
    We can use any of the models above to show this property for specific \(a,b\) and \(c\text{.}\) For example to
    \begin{equation*} 5 \times (3 + 4) = 5 \times 3 + 5 \times 4 \end{equation*}
    we can use the following diagram:

    The overall number of squares is \(5 \times (3+4)\text{.}\) The individual colors are \(5 \times 3\) and \(5 \times 4\text{.}\)

    Subsection 2.4.3 Thinking Strategies for Multiplication

    As with the previous operations, there are some important ways to think about multiplication. There are a few concepts.
    🔗

    Addition Doubles.

    This uses the knowledge of doubles like \(2+2=4, 5+5=10\) to introduce multiplication by 2.
    🔗
    🔗

    Skip Counting.

    This starts students in understanding multiplication consider skip counting by 2, 3 and 5 such as
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, \ldots \\ 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, \ldots \\ 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, \ldots \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    and these are precisely multiplying by 2, 3 and 5.
    🔗
    🔗

    Multiplication Table.

    Eventually, students need to know the multiplication table within 12. This should be introduced slowly, using skip counting and the knowledge of the easier rows.
    🔗
    🔗

    Multiplying by tens or multiples of 10s.

    A place-value system like decimal numbers make multiplying by powers of 10 quite easy.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}3 \times 10\amp= \text{3 tens}= 30, \\ 42 \times 10\amp= \text{42 tens}= 420, \\ 8 \times 100\amp= \text{8 hundreds}= 800, \\ 192 \times 10\amp= (\text{1 hundred}+ \text{9 tens}+ \text{2 ones}) \times 10 \\\amp= (\text{10 hundreds}+ \text{9 hundreds}+ \text{2 tens}) \\\amp= 1920\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
