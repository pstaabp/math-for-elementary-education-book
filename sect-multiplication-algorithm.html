    Section 5.3 Multiplication Algorithms

    Objectives

    • Understand the expanded form of multiplication.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Understand the Box Model of multiplication
      🔗
      🔗
    • Understand the chip model of multiplication.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use the chip model to understand the column algorithm.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Understand the lattice model of multiplication.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Perform base-5 multiplication with both a chip model and the column algorithm.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The goal of the multiplication algorithm is to multiply numbers that are difficult to do with mental math.
    🔗
    Consider first, a multiplication like \(7 \times 64\text{.}\) This doesn’t fit into any mental math problem, so we will develop an algorithm to solve this. In short, this will include:
    🔗
    • writing the second factor in expanded form, \(64 = 60 + 4\text{,}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • using the distributive property, \(7 \times (60 + 4) = 7 \times 60 + 7 \times 4\text{,}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • shifting place values when multiplying by 10,
      🔗
      🔗
    • using the 1-digit multiplication facts,
      🔗
      🔗
    • bundling with the addition algorithm.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.1 Stages to Learning Multiplication

    Multiplication Facts
    🔗
    As discussed, the multiplication table up to \(9 \times 9\) must be known. This was seen in Chapter 2.
    🔗
    Mental Multiplication
    🔗
    This helps bypass the more-complicated general multiplication algorithm.
    🔗
    1-digit Multiplication with no Bundling
    🔗
    We can start with an example like \(3 \times 13\) and recall that this is the same as \(13+13+13\) and can be examined with coin, chip or DLB models.
    🔗
    1-digit Multiplication with Bundling
    🔗
    We first start with examples like \(4 \times 23\) or \(6 \times 25\text{.}\) This can be done with both chip models and the expand form as shown below.
    🔗
    What we’ve done here is use the distributive property of numbers in the following way:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 4 \times 13 \amp = 4 \times (10 + 3) = 4 \times 10 + 4 \times 3 \\ \amp = 40 + 12 = 52. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The Full Algorithm
    🔗
    We will soon look at the problem \(32 \times 27\text{.}\) The main idea behind the full algorithm is to break up one of the factors into its basic bundles. That is \(27 = 20 + 7\text{.}\) Then we will perform the operation \(32 \times 20\) and add it to \(32 \times 7\text{.}\) The only difference here is that we need to recall that \(32 \times 20=32 \times 2 \times 10\) and multiplying by 10 shifts the value by including a 0 at the end.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.2 Expanded Form of Multiplication

    We won’t use a chip model for this because of the size of the numbers makes for a larger number of chips. Instead, we will modify the column addition algorithm.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}\begin{array}{rrl}\amp 3 2 \\ \times \amp 2 7 \\ \hline \amp 14 \amp \text{ $\leftarrow 7 \times 2$} \\ \amp 210 \amp \text{$\leftarrow 7 \times 30$} \\ \amp 40 \amp \text{ $\leftarrow 20 \times 2$} \\ + \amp 600 \amp \text{ $\leftarrow 20 \times 30$} \\ \hline \amp 864\end{array}\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Notice that in this case, there are 4 single-digit multiplications (that you should do with math facts). Some of them include a multiply by 10 as well. Then the result is that these four numbers need to be added.
    🔗
    The reason this method works is due to the distributive property written out in a different form.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned}32 \times 27\amp= (30+2) \times (20 + 7)\amp\amp\text{distribute over second term}\\\amp= (30 +2 ) \times 20 + (30 + 2) \times 7\amp\amp\text{distribute over first term}\\\amp= 30 \times 20 + 2 \times 20 + 30 \times 7 + 2 \times 7 \\\amp= 600 + 40 + 210 + 14 = 864\end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The two distributive properties in the first two steps are also commonly known as FOILing for (First-Outer-Inner-Last) that can be done in one step.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.3 Box Model of Multiplication

    An alternative way to look at the expanded form of multiplication is called the box model in which the numbers are written outside the a grid. For example:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{matrix} \amp \begin{matrix} 30 \amp 2 \end{matrix} \amp \\ \begin{matrix} 20 \\ 7 \end{matrix} \amp \begin{array}{|c|c|}\hline \phantom{600} \amp \phantom{40} \\ \hline \phantom{210} \amp \phantom{45} \\ \hline \end{array} \amp \begin{matrix} \\ \end{matrix} \end{matrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Then the products are place inside the grid:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{matrix} \amp \begin{matrix} 30 \amp 2 \end{matrix} \amp \\ \begin{matrix} 20 \\ 7 \end{matrix} \amp \begin{array}{|c|c|}\hline 600 \amp 40 \\ \hline 210 \amp 14\\ \hline \end{array} \amp \begin{matrix} \\ \end{matrix} \end{matrix} \end{equation*}
    and notice that the 4 numbers inside the grid are the same 4 products in the expanded form about in the sum section.
    🔗
    We now just total all four numbers. If this is too much, you can add across first
    \begin{equation*} \begin{matrix} \amp \begin{matrix} 30 \amp 2 \end{matrix} \amp \\ \begin{matrix} 20 \\ 7 \end{matrix} \amp \begin{array}{|c|c|}\hline 600 \amp 40 \\ \hline 210 \amp 14\\ \hline \end{array} \amp \begin{matrix} 640 \\ 224 \end{matrix} \end{matrix} \end{equation*}
    and then add down to get the result 864.
    🔗
    This model is useful to understand place value and multiplication and then realized that after the 1-digit multiplication that the result is the sum of (in this case) for numbers.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.4 Chip Models with Multiplication

    Chip models are helpful in understanding how the column algorithm works and since multiplication is just repeated addition, an understanding of the chip model of addition is all that is needed. The following example shows a relatively simple multiplication problem:
    🔗

    Example 5.3.1.

    Show a chip model of \(4 \times 13\text{.}\) Also show the column algorithm and explain the connection between them.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We will start with a simple 1-digit multiplication problem, \(4 \times 13\text{,}\) which will show as \(13+13+13+13\) with the following. First start with 4 groups of 13 on the chart:
    🔗
    As with the chip model in addition, we need to bundle 10 of the ones into 1 ten chip.
    🔗
    and this shows that there are 5 tens and 2 ones or 52.
    🔗
    Next, we examine this using column addition.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rrr}\amp 1 \amp 3 \\ \times \amp \amp 4 \\ \hline\end{array} \end{equation*}
    First multiply the 3 and the 4. The result, \(12\) and put the ones digit below the line (in the ones column) and the tens digit above the top number.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rrr}\amp {\scriptstyle 1} \amp \\ \amp 1 \amp 3 \\ \times \amp \amp 4 \\ \hline \amp \amp 2\end{array} \end{equation*}
    Next, multiply the 4 and the 1 to get a 4. Add the 1 from the attic to get a total of 5.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rrr}\amp {\scriptstyle 1} \amp \\ \amp 1 \amp 3 \\ \times \amp \amp 4 \\ \hline \amp 5 \amp 2\end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The next chip model example shows a more complicated chip model.
    🔗

    Example 5.3.2.

    Show the chip model for \(6 \times 367\) as well as the column algorithm and explain the connection.
    🔗
    Solution.
    First start with 6 groups of 367 and we will need a 1000s column, so we include it from the beginning.
    🔗
    As with the addition algorithm, we start by bundling the ones.
    🔗
    Now we’ll bundle the tens:
    🔗
    and next we bundle the 100s:
    🔗
    And this shows that the result is 2 thousands, 2 hundreds, 0 tens and 2 ones or 2,202.
    🔗
    Now let’s look at the column algorithm for this. Start with 6 on the bottom row and 367 on the top row:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rrrrr} \amp\amp3 \amp 6 \amp 7 \\ \times\amp \amp \amp \amp 6 \\ \hline \end{array} \end{equation*}
    the first steps is to do \(6 \times 7=42\text{,}\) write the ones digits (2) below the line and the tens digit (4) above the top row,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rrrrr} \amp\amp\amp {\scriptstyle 4} \amp \\ \amp\amp 3 \amp 6 \amp 7 \\ \times\amp \amp \amp \amp 6 \\ \hline \amp\amp\amp\amp 2 \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Next, do the tens column or \(6 \times 6=36\) and add the 4 above the top line to get \(40\text{.}\) Write the ones digits (0) below the line in the tens digit and the 4 above the 3 in the hundreds digit.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rrrrr} \amp\amp{\scriptstyle 4} \amp{\scriptstyle 4} \amp \\ \amp\amp 3 \amp 6 \amp 7 \\ \times\amp \amp \amp \amp 6 \\ \hline \amp\amp\amp0 \amp 2 \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Next, do the hundreds column \(6 \times 3 = 18\) and add 4 to get 22. Since this is the last step, write 22 below the line
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rrrrr} \amp\amp{\scriptstyle 4} \amp{\scriptstyle 4} \amp \\ \amp\amp 3 \amp 6 \amp 7 \\ \times\amp \amp \amp \amp 6 \\ \hline \amp2 \amp 2 \amp0 \amp 2 \end{array} \end{equation*}
    And the result is the same.
    🔗

    🔗

    0
    1
    2
