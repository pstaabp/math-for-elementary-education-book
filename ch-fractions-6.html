    Section 7.4 Division of Fractions

    Although dividing two fractions in proper or improper form is quite straightforward, division is difficult in terms of word problems. So we start with a reminder of the difference between partitive and measurement division.
    🔗

    Subsection 7.4.1 Review of Partitive and Measurement Division

    Measurement Division
    🔗
    If you know the size, and are determining the number of parts. 15 is how many of size 3?
    🔗
    Partitive Division
    🔗
    If you know the number of parts and are determining the size. 15 is 3 groups of what size?
    🔗
    🔗
    Also remember that the following bar diagrams are very useful for these:
    🔗
    Measurement Division
    🔗
    This shows \(15 \div 3\) with measurement division interpretation.

    🔗
    This shows \(15 \div 3\) with a measurement division interpretation.

    Note 7.4.1. Partitive Word Problems.

    The following is a number of word problems that are partitive. Remember that the important part is that the divisor represents the number of parts.
    🔗
    • Eddie has 15 marbles. He puts them equally in 3 bags. How many marbles are in each bag?
      🔗
      🔗

      🔗
      🔗

      🔗
      🔗

      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Note 7.4.2. Measurement Word Problems.

    The following is a number of word problems that are measurement. Remember that the important part is that the divisor represents the size of each part.
    🔗

      🔗
      🔗

      🔗
      🔗
    • 5 is 3/4 of what number?
      🔗
      🔗
    • Tallulah rode her bike to her Grandmother’s house. She stopped 3 miles along the way and was 3/4 of the way to her Grandmother’s house. When she got to the house, how far did she ride?
      🔗
      🔗
    • Mr. Taylor is building a fence. Each board is 5/6 of foot. How many boards does he need to build a fence that is 13 1/3 feet long?
      🔗
      🔗
    • A rope of length \(3 \frac{3}{4}\) feet is cut into pieces of length \(\frac{3}{4}\) of a foot. How many pieces of rope were there?

    Subsection 7.4.7 Fraction Division Rule

    The last arithmetic operation we have not seen as a rule is that of division.
    🔗

    Property 7.4.11. Fraction Division.

    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \frac{a}{b}\div \frac{c}{b}\amp = a \div c\amp \amp \text{or}\amp \frac{a}{b}\div \frac{c}{b}\amp = \frac{a}{c} \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This rule can actually be used for any division of fractions and notice that there are two interpretations. The one on the left results in another division, the one on the right is a fraction. Either is correct and which to use often depends on the context.
    🔗

    Proof of the Fraction Division Rule.
    We can interpret \(\frac{a}{b}\div \frac{c}{b}\) using a measurement division. That is, let \(\frac{a}{b}\) be divided into pieces of size \(\frac{c}{b}\text{.}\) The total number of pieces is \(\frac{a}{b}\div \frac{c}{b}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If we rescaled the picture, to a total length of \(a\) and piece size \(c\text{,}\) the number of pieces is the same. That is, the number of pieces is \(a \div c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This shows that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a}{b}\div \frac{c}{b} = a \div c \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.4.12.

    Use Property 7.4.11 to evaluate the following division problems:
    🔗
    (a)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{10}{3} \div \frac{2}{3} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the Fraction Division property
    \begin{equation*} \frac{10}{3} \div \frac{2}{3} = 10 \div 2 = 5 \end{equation*}
    and note that this is the same as Example 7.4.10.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{3}{4}\div \dfrac{1}{6} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Note that to use Property 7.4.11, a common denominator must be found. This is \(12\) and we write both \(\frac{3}{4}\) and \(\frac{1}{6}\) with a denominator of 12. Since \(\frac{3}{4}= \frac{9}{12}\) and \(\frac{1}{6}=\frac{2}{12}\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{3}{4}\div \frac{1}{6}= \frac{9}{12}\div \frac{2}{12}= \frac{9}{2} \end{equation*}
    where we have used the fraction version of division in this case since 2 does not divide 9.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We will see a more general division rule below, but first some other terminology.
    🔗

    Definition 7.4.13.

    Recall that the reciprocal of a fraction is the fraction found by switching the numerator and denominator. It is also called the inverse of the fraction
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.4.14.

    • The reciprocal of \(\frac{2}{3}\) is \(\frac{3}{2}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The reciprocal of \(\frac{1}{3}\) is \(\frac{3}{1}=3\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The reciprocal of \(4=\frac{4}{1}\) is \(\frac{1}{4}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The next property uses a different format of fraction division and is more general than Property 7.4.11 because a common denominator does not need to be found.
    🔗

    Property 7.4.15. Fraction Division: Invert and Multiply.

    Let \(a,b,c\) and \(d\) be positive whole numbers,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a}{b}\div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b}\times \frac{d}{c} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Explaining the Invert and Multiply Rule.

    Why does this work? We use a particular example with partitive interpretation, measurement interpretation and some prealgebra to help explain this property.
    🔗
    Partitive Interpretation
    🔗
    Consider the problem 12 is \(\frac{2}{3}\) of what number which is the operation \(12 \div \frac{2}{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This can be solved by considering that the number is \(\frac{1}{3}\) of the number is 6, then the number is 18. This means that \(12 \div \frac{2}{3}\) can be found by first multiplying \(12\) by \(\frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) then multiplying by 3 or
    \begin{equation*} 12 \div \frac{2}{3}= 12 \times \frac{3}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Measurement Interpretation
    🔗
    How many units of size \(\frac{2}{3}\) is 12. Counting in thirds, \(12\) is 36 thirds, so how many units of size \(2\) is 36. The answer is 18.
    🔗
    This is the same as changing units:
    🔗
    In both cases, we have shown that
    \begin{equation*} 12 \div \frac{2}{3} = 12 \times 3 \times \frac{1}{2}= 12 \times \frac{3}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Prealgebra
    🔗
    Recall that the division problem \(12 \div \frac{2}{3}\) can be thought of as \(\frac{2}{3}\) times something equals 12 or
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \frac{2}{3}x\amp = 12 \\ \frac{3}{2}\cdot \frac{2}{3}x \amp = \frac{3}{2}\times 12 \\ x\amp = \frac{3}{2}\times 12 \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.4.10 2-step fraction word problems

    A 2-step word problem with fractions can be difficult. Here is a couple of examples with these. Note: in both of these examples we use a unitary method, however, the unit can change.
    🔗

    Example 7.4.19. 2-step Teacher’s Solution.

    Develop a Teacher’s solution of this problem: 3/5 of Sollie’s marble collection are blue. She gave 3/4 of her blue marbles to her sister which is 45 marbles. How many marbles did Sollie start with?
    🔗
    Solution.
    We first start with showing a bar diagram with 3/5 (which represents the blue marbles).
    🔗
    To solve this, we use the unitary method keeping track of the top and bottom units are different:
    🔗
    Bottom Diagram
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 3 \text{ units} \amp = 45 \qquad \div 3 \\ 1 \text{unit} \amp = 15 \qquad \times 4 \\ 4 \text{ units} \amp = 60 \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Top Diagram
    🔗
    Since the full bar on the bottom is identical to 3 units on the top bar then:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 3\text{ units} \amp = 60 \qquad \div 3 \\ 1\text{ unit} \amp = 20 \qquad \times 5 \\ 5 \text{ units} \amp = 100 \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So the top bar is 100.
    🔗
    🔗
    Sollie had 100 marbles to start with.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.4.20.

    Develop a Teacher’s Solution of the following problem. A roomful of people watched a chess match between Angela and Bryce. 4/11 of the spectators favored Angela and the rest favored Bryce. If 12 more people favored Bryce over Angela, how many spectators were in the room?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Note that there are a total of 11 squares and there are 4 assigned to Angela. This represents \(\frac{4}{11}\text{.}\) The remainder or \(\frac{7}{11}\) are assigned to Bryce.
    🔗
    We now use the unitary method to solve this:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 3\text{ units}\amp = 12 \quad \div 3 \\ 1 \text{ unit}\amp = 4 \quad \times 11 \\ 11 \text{ units}\amp = 44 \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    There are 44 spectators in the room.
