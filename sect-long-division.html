    Section 5.4 Long Division

    Objectives

    • Use a coin and chip model to perform division with 1-digit divisors.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Understand the difference between partitive and measurement approve for long division.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Perform the long division algorithm and understand the relationship to the quotient remainder theorem.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use tables or estimation to find long division problems with 2 or more digit divisors.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Perform the long division algorithm in base-5 or other non decimal bases.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    In the time of calculators everywhere, why do we need to learn the long division algorithm to perform a calculation that can take a long time? Well there are three key reasons:
    🔗
    1. It relates fractions and decimals (we’ll see this in detail in Chapter 10 ). It’s key in understanding irrational numbers.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. It illustrates the notion of a successive approximation and an iterative algorithm. We will see that each step of long division gets closer to the solution.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. It is used in Precalculus to factor polynomials finding partial fractions.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The curriculum sequence for division clearly starts with 1-digit divisors before 2 or more digit divisors.
    🔗

    Subsection 5.4.1 Long Division with 1-digit divisors

    Prerequisites.

    The following are needed for long division by a 1-digit whole number:
    🔗
    1. A solid understanding of place value.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. A solid knowledge of the meaning of multiplication and the basic multiplication facts to \(10 \times 10\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. A solid understanding of the meaning of division (both partitive and measurement).
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Multi-digit subtraction with regrouping.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Understanding that \(3)\!\!\overline{\;15}\) means \(15 \div 3\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    The Partitive Approach.

    We start with a partitive-division word problem to explain the steps. As we will see, coin models and examples are helpful for starting. Consider the following example:
    🔗
    Example 5.4.1.
    José wants to put 73 cents into 3 separate jars so each jar has the same number of candies. How many candies go in each? How many candies are left over?
    🔗
    🔗
    We will use a coin model to understand this problem first. Consider 7 dimes and 1 penny, which will look like this:
    🔗
    Since we would like to put these coins in three separate jars, we start with the 10s (dimes). There are 7 of these and the best we can do is 3 jars of 2 dimes each.
    🔗
    To get the remaining coins into the 3 jars, we unbundle the remaining dime into 10 pennies:
    🔗
    The next step is to take the 13 1s coins and put them in 3 piles (jars).
    🔗
    And this shows that there are 3 groups of 2 dimes and 4 pennies in each pile/jar with 1 penny left over. This shows that \(73 \div 3 = 24 R 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We now look at this in look at the long division version of \(73 \div 3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 1:
    🔗
    We start with the division bracket (also called the division house or division box):
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 3 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;7\;\;3\;\;} \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The divisor goes to the left side and the dividend on the inside.
    🔗
    Step 2:
    🔗
    We need to do division from largest place value to smallest (from left to right), so the start is to find \(7 \div 3\text{.}\) With some mental math this is 2 and we write this number above the 7.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2\;\;\phantom{3} \; \; \amp\\ 3 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\; 7 \; \; 3\; \;} \amp \; \; \\ \underline{\; 6 \; \; \phantom{8} \; \; } \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Note that the 2 is the number of dimes in each of the piles in the coin model above. The 6 is the total number of dimes in the 3 piles.
    🔗
    Step 3:
    🔗
    Next, we subtract \(7-6\) and bring down the 3 in the dividend:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2\;\;\phantom{3} \; \; \amp \\ 3 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\; 7 \; \; 1\; \;} \amp \; \; \\ \underline{\; 6 \; \; \phantom{8} \; \; } \amp \; \; \\ 1 \; \; 3 \; \; \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The "13" in the bottom row consists of two parts. The 1 is the number of 10s left over after dividing the 7 10s into 3 piles. The 3 is the number of 1s from the dividend (we just brought it down). The 13 is the total number of 1s after unbundling.
    🔗
    Step 4:
    🔗
    Next, we determine the number of pennies that can be put in each pile of size 3. This is \(13 \div 4\) and the 4 goes in the top in the ones column.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2\;\;4 \; \; \amp \\ 3 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\; 7 \; \; 3\; \;} \amp \; \; \\ \underline{\; 6 \; \; \phantom{8} \; \; } \amp \\ 1 \; \; 3 \; \; \amp \;\; \\ 1 \; \; 2 \; \; \amp \; \; \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Note that the 4 in the ones column on the top row (quotient) is the number of 1s in each pile/jar and the 12 in the bottom row is the total number of 1s in the 3 piles/jars ( \(3 \times 4\) ).
    🔗
    Step 5:
    🔗
    Lastly, just subtract.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2\;\;4 \; \; \amp \\ 3 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\; 7 \; \; 3\; \;} \amp \; \; \\ \underline{\; 6 \; \; \phantom{8} \; \; } \amp \\ 1 \; \; 3 \; \; \amp \;\; \\ \underline{1 \; \; 2 \; \;} \amp \\ 1 \; \; \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    And the bottom row is the remainder.
    🔗
    🔗
    In short, the long division is the shorthand version of the coin model.
    🔗
    The following is another example, however, we will use a chip model instead:
    🔗
    Example 5.4.2.
    Find \(735 \div 4\) using a chip model and the corresponding long division. That is think of 7 dollar coins , 3 dimes and 5 pennies divided between 4 people.
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, we’ll put the chips on the chart.
    🔗
    Since we are dividing these into 4 groups, we’ll make 4 rows in the chart to move the chips:
    🔗
    The next step is to move 100s chips into the rows below. Only 1 will fit in each and we’ll cross out the results:
    🔗
    Then we need to unbundle the remaining three 100s chips and move into the 10s columns to equally spread.
    🔗
    For the next step, we move two chips from the top row of the 10s column into the bottom two rows to make equal sized rows:
    🔗
    Next, we unbundle the remaining tens chip to the ones column to equally space into the rows:
    🔗
    The last step is to move 2 of the chips in the ones column on the top row to the bottom two rows:
    🔗
    And now we are done. We have successfully moved the chips from the top row to the bottom four rows so each of the bottom four rows have an equal number in each, which has 1 hundreds chip, 8 tens chips and 5 ones chips. There are 3 ones chips left. This shows that \(735 \div 4 = 185 \; R3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We now show the long division and make the connections between the chip model and the long division. First, we’ll start with the basic setup of the long division:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \phantom{1} \; \; \phantom{0} \; \; \phantom{9} \; \; \amp \; \; \\ 4 \; | \overline{\; 7 \; \; 3 \; \; 5 \; \; } \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Just the first step in the chip model, we move 1 hundreds chip into each of each of the 4 rows below it. This one (in the hundreds place of the quotient in blue) represents this.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} {\color{MaterialBlue}1} \; \; \phantom{0} \; \; \phantom{9} \; \; \amp \; \; \\ 4 \; | \overline{\; 7 \; \; 3 \; \; 5 \; \; } \amp \\ \underline{\; 4 \; \; \phantom{0} \; \; \phantom{0} \; \;} \amp \qquad \text{total number of hundreds chips in the four rows} \\ {\color{MaterialRed900}3} \; \; \phantom{0} \; \; \phantom{0} \; \; \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Also, shown is the next step in long division is the subtraction of \(7-4=3\) in the hundreds column and is in red above. This represents the remainder in \(7 \div 4\) and is the chips remaining in the hundreds column couldn’t be equally divided into the four rows.
    🔗
    This is repeated now for the tens column. The substeps here are:
    🔗
    1. The 3 from the tens column of the dividend is brought down next to the remainder from the hundreds chips. This is in orange and represents the total number of tens chips after unbundling the remaining hundreds chips.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Then \(33 \div 4 = 8 \; R\;1\) is done.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The quotient 8 (in blue) is written on the top line in the tens columns. The product \(4 \times 8 = 32\) is written below the 33 and the 1 (in red) is the remainder.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} {1} \; \; {\color{MidnightBlue}8} \; \; \phantom{9} \; \; \amp \qquad\text{The 8 is the number of groups of size 4} \\ 4 \; | \overline{\; 7 \; \; 3 \; \; 5 \; } \amp \\ \underline{\; 4 \;} \; \phantom{0} \; \; \phantom{0} \; \amp \\ {\color{BurntOrange}3} \; \; {\color{BurntOrange}3} \; \; \phantom{0} \; \amp \qquad\text{total number of tens chips after unbundling} \\ \underline{\; 3 \; \; 2 \; }\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \qquad \text{total number of tens chips in groups of 4. } \\ {\color{BrickRed} 1} \;\;\phantom{0}\;\amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This is now repeated for the ones column:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} {1} \; \; 8 \; \; {\color{MidnightBlue} 3} \; \; \amp \qquad\text{The 3 is the number of groups of size 4} \\ 4 \; | \overline{\; 7 \; \; 3 \; \; 5 \; \; } \amp \\ \underline{\; 4 \; }\; \phantom{0} \; \; \phantom{0} \; \; \amp \\ 3 \; \; 3 \; \; \phantom{0} \; \; \amp \\ \underline{\; 3 \; \; 2 \; \;\phantom{0} \;\;} \amp \\ {\color{BurntOrange}1} \;\;{\color{BurntOrange}5}\;\;\amp \qquad\text{total number of ones chips after unbundling} \\ \underline{\; 1\;\;2\;\;} \amp \qquad \text{total number of ones chips in groups of 4. } \\ 3 \; \; \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Measurement approach.

    Above we examined division using the partitive interpretation. We also can examine long division using the measurement interpretation. Let’s first start with a simple example in which long division is a bit overkill to illustrate the point.
    🔗
    Consider \(20 \div 6\text{,}\) which is interpreted in the measurement sense, as 20 items put in groups of size 6. The following diagram uses the number line to demonstrate this:
    🔗
    One can think of a rope of length 20 inches and need to be cut into pieces of length 6. The long division algorithm:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 3 \;\; \amp \; \; \text{number of segments} \\ 6\; )\!\!\overline{\;\;2 \; \; 0}\;\;\amp \; \; \\ \underline{\;1 \; \; 8 \; \;} \amp \; \; \text{total lengths of 3 segments of length 6} \\ 2 \; \; \amp \; \; \text{distance remaining} \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Example 5.4.3.
    Find \(665 \div 5\) using the measurement approach.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since it is near impossible to write the number of segments of length 5 that go into 665, we will take another approach. We’ll use some mental math first.
    🔗
    • We know that \(5 \times 100 = 500\text{,}\) so one hundred segments of length 5 makes it to 500.
      🔗
      🔗
    • There is 165 left over.
      🔗
      🔗
    • We know that \(5 \times 30 = 150\) so another 30 segments of length 5 goes another 150.
      🔗
      🔗
    • There is 15 left over.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(5 \times 3 = 15\text{,}\) so it takes another 3 segments. Nothing is left over.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The total number of segments is 133.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The long division shows this as well:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 1 \; \; 3 \; \; 3 \; \; \amp \\ 5 \; )\!\! \overline{\;\; 6 \; \; 6 \; \; 5 \;} \; \amp \\ \underline{ \; 5 \; \; 0 \; \; 0 \; \; } \amp \; \; \text{total length of 1 hundred segments} \\ \; 1 \; \; 6 \; \; 5 \; \; \amp \; \; \text{165 units left over} \\ \underline{\; 1 \; \; 5 \; \; 0 \; \;} \amp \; \; \text{total length of 30 segments of length 5} \\ 1 \; \; 5 \; \; \amp \; \; \text{15 units left over} \\ \underline{\; 1 \; \; 5 \; \; } \amp \; \; \text{3 segments of length 5} \\ 0 \; \; \amp \; \; \text{nothing left} \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.4.2 The long division algorithm and the Quotient Remainder Theorem

    This section should also be titled, Why Does Long Division Work?. As discussed earlier in this text, as teachers, we need to know the why things work, not just how they work. Let’s look at the example above: \(735 \div 4\text{,}\) which again, we can write using long division as
    🔗
    In short, the why this works is a combination of place value and the Quotient Remainder Theorem. Let’s look at the long division algorithm for this problem.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 1 \; \; 8 \; \; 3 \; \; \amp \\ 4 \; )\!\! \overline{\;\; 7 \; \; 3 \; \; 5 \;} \; \amp \\ \underline{ \; 4 \; \; 0 \; \; 0 \; \; } \amp \; \; \text{total length of 1 hundred segments} \\ \; 3 \; \; 3 \; \; 5 \; \; \amp \; \; \text{383 units left over} \\ \underline{\; 3 \; \; 2 \; \; 0 \; \;} \amp \; \; \text{total length of 80 segments of length 4} \\ 1 \; \; 5 \; \; \amp \; \; \text{15 units left over} \\ \underline{\; 1 \; \; 2 \; \; } \amp \; \; \text{3 segments of length 4} \\ 3 \; \; \amp \; \; \text{remainder} \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    1. We first do the division on the hundreds digit: \(7 \div 4 = 1 \; R\;3\text{.}\) From the QR theorem, this implies that \(1 \times 4 +3 = 7\text{.}\) Multiplying this through by 100
      \begin{equation*} 100 \times 4 + 300 = 700 \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Then we look at the tens step which is \(33 \div 4 = 8 \; R\;1\) or
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 8 \times 4 + 1\amp= 33\amp\amp\text{multiply through by 10}\\ 80 \times 4 + 10\amp= 330 \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Lastly, the ones step is \(15 \div 4 = 3 \; R 3\) which can be written:
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 4 \times 3 + 1\amp= 15 \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Combine these
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 735\amp= (400 + 335) \\ \amp= 4 \times 100 + (320 + 15) \\ \amp= 4 \times 100 + 4 \times 80 + 4 \times 3 + 3 \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.4.3 Extending the Long Division method to multiple-digit divisors

    There isn’t anything different with the long division method for larger divisors. It just gets a bit more difficult. Often to make it easier, one can develop a table of multiples of the divisor. The following example shows this.
    🔗

    Example 5.4.4.

    Find \(12989 \div 21\) by first finding a table of multiples of 21 and then using the table.
    🔗
    Solution.
    First consider the table of multiples of 21. And this can be generated reasonably easier by successively adding 21 to the previous value.
    🔗
    \(1 \times 21 = 21\) \(4 \times 21 = 84\) \(7 \times 21 = 147\)
    \(2 \times 21 = 42\) \(5 \times 21 = 105\) \(8 \times 21 = 168\)
    \(3 \times 21 = 63\) \(6 \times 21 = 126\) \(9 \times 21 = 189\)
    Next, we’ll walk through the long division algorithm using the table. First, we are looking for \(129 \div 21\) and looking at the table we given that \(6\times 21=126\text{,}\) so we put 6 in the hundreds column of the quotient and the product (126) below the 129 and then also find the remainder.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 6\;\;\phantom{7}\;\;\phantom{4} \; \amp \\ 21 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;1\;\; 2 \; \; 9 \; \; 8\; \; 9\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;1 \; \; 2 \;\;6\;} \; \phantom{2}\;\; \phantom{6}\; \amp \\ 3 \; \; \phantom{5} \; \; \phantom{6} \; \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The next step, we’ll bring the 8 from the tens column of the dividend down and then we want to do \(38 \div 21 \text{.}\) From the table, this is 1 and the remainder will be 14. Put the quotient (1) on the top in the tens column, the product \(1 \times 21\) below the 35 and the remainder below that.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 6\;\;1\;\;\phantom{4} \; \amp \\ 21 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;1\;\; 2 \; \; 9 \; \; 8\; \; 9\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;1 \; \; 2 \;\;6\;} \; \phantom{2}\;\; \phantom{6}\; \amp \\ 3 \; \; 8 \; \; \phantom{6} \; \amp \\ \underline{\; 2 \; \; 1 \; } \; \phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 1 \; \; 7 \; \; \phantom{0} \; \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Just bring the 9 (ones digit of the dividend) down:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 6\;\;1\;\;\phantom{8} \; \amp \\ 21 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;1\;\; 2 \; \; 9 \; \; 8\; \; 9\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;1 \; \; 2 \;\;6\;} \; \phantom{2}\;\; \phantom{6}\; \amp \\ 3 \; \; 8 \; \; \phantom{6} \; \amp \\ \underline{\; 2 \; \; 1 \; } \; \phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 1 \; \; 7 \; \; 9 \; \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For the last step (the ones), we seek \(179 \div 21\text{.}\) Using the table, since 179 is not a product in the table, go to the lower one and find \(21 \times 8 = 168\text{.}\) Thus the quotient of \(179 \div 21\) is 8 and the remainder is \(179-168=11\text{.}\) These all go on the long division diagram.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 6\;\;1\;\;8 \; \amp \\ 21 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;1\;\; 2 \; \; 9 \; \; 8\; \; 9\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;1 \; \; 2 \;\;6\;} \; \phantom{2}\;\; \phantom{6}\; \amp \\ 3 \; \; 8 \; \; \phantom{6} \; \amp \\ \underline{\; 2 \; \; 1 \; } \; \phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 1 \; \; 7 \; \; 9 \; \amp \\ \underline{\; 1 \; \; 6 \; \; 8} \; \amp \\ 1 \; \; 1 \; \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This is done because there is nothing left to bring down from the dividend. This shows that \(12989 \div 21 = 618 \; R \; 11\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Also, as a check, we use multiplication to see if this is correct.
    \begin{equation*} 618 \times 21 + 11 = 12978 + 11 = 12989 \end{equation*}
    So the division was correct. Note: the multiplication algorithm was used here but not shown.

    Subsection 5.4.4 Long Division using estimation

    Although this is nice, it requires that we create a multiplication table (of size 9). This may take longer than actually performing the division.
    🔗
    Instead, we will use estimation to do this. Consider \(90820 \div 49\text{.}\) This one is particularly nice in that the divisor is close to 50 and it is fairly easy to estimate dividing by 50.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \phantom{1}\;\;\phantom{8}\;\;\phantom{5} \;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 49 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;9\;\; 0 \; \; 8 \; \; 2\; \; 0\;} \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The first step will be to determine how many 49 go into 90 and two 49s is too many, so 1 is correct.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 1\;\;\phantom{8}\;\;\phantom{5} \;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 49 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;9\;\; 0 \; \; 8 \; \; 2\; \; 0\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;4 \; \; 9 \;} \; \phantom{2}\;\; \phantom{6}\;\; \phantom{6}\; \amp \\ 4 \;\; 1 \; \; 8 \;\;\phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    And the subtraction \(90-49\) has been shown. Then next, the \(8\) is combined with the \(41\text{,}\) so the next step is \(418 \div 49\text{.}\) From Section 4.3, we handled problems like this. Since \(418 \div 49 \approx 400 \div 50 = 800 \div 100 = 8\text{,}\) where the last step is compensation.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 1\;\;8\;\;\phantom{5} \;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 49 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;9\;\; 0 \; \; 8 \; \; 2\; \; 0\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;4 \; \; 9 \;} \; \phantom{2}\;\; \phantom{6}\;\; \phantom{6}\; \amp \\ 4 \;\; 1 \; \; 8 \;\;\phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ \underline{\;3\;\;9\;\;2\;} \; \phantom{0} \; \; \phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 2 \; \; 6 \; \;2 \; \; \phantom{0} \; \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and again a little mental math for \(5 \times 49 = 245\) is used. The last step will bring the 0 down to result in the problem \(170 \div 49\text{,}\) which you can find to have the quotient 3.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 1\;\;8\;\;5 \;\;3 \; \amp \\ 49 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;9\;\; 0 \; \; 8 \; \; 2\; \; 0\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;4 \; \; 9 \;} \; \phantom{2}\;\; \phantom{6}\;\; \phantom{6}\; \amp \\ 4 \;\; 1 \; \; 8 \;\;\phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ \underline{\;3\;\;9\;\;2\;} \; \phantom{0} \; \; \phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 2 \; \; 6 \; \;2 \; \; \phantom{0} \; \amp \\ \underline{\; 2 \;\; 4 \; \; 5\;} \;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ 1 \;\;7\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ \underline{\;1\;\;4\;\;7\;} \amp \\ 2\;\;3\; \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The next example is a little more complicated, but uses the same skills.
    🔗

    Example 5.4.5.

    Find \(195894 \div 721\) using estimation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Start with the basics of the long division.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \phantom{2}\;\;\phantom{1}\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 721 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;1\;\; 9 \; \; 5 \; \; 8\; \; 9\;\;4\;} \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    To determine the first quotient, we seek a number larger than the divisor. This number is 1958. We seek a approximation for \(1958 \div 721 \approx 2000 \div 700\text{.}\) It appears that 3 is too large and we’ll try 2.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2\;\;\phantom{1}\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 721 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;1\;\; 9 \; \; 5 \; \; 8\; \; 9\;\;4\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;1 \; \; 4 \;\;4\;\;2\;} \; \phantom{2}\;\; \phantom{6}\;\amp \\ 5 \;\; 1 \; \; 6 \;\;\phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and recall that when we perform the subtraction note that 516 is smaller than 721, so the 2 in the quotient was correct. (If the bottom number was larger than 721, then 3 should be used instead.) Bring down the 9:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2\;\;\phantom{1}\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 721 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;1\;\; 9 \; \; 5 \; \; 8\; \; 9\;\;4\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;1 \; \; 4 \;\;4\;\;2\;} \; \phantom{2}\;\; \phantom{6}\;\amp \\ 5 \;\; 1 \; \; 6 \;\;9\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    And now we do \(5169 \div 721 \approx 5200 \div 700\) which is about 7. Let’s try that.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2\;\;7\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 721 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;1\;\; 9 \; \; 5 \; \; 8\; \; 9\;\;4\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;1 \; \; 4 \;\;4\;\;2\;} \; \phantom{2}\;\; \phantom{6}\;\amp \\ 5 \;\; 1 \; \; 6 \;\;9\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ \underline{5\;\;0\;\; 4 \; \; 7 \; } \; \phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 1\;\;2 \; \; 2 \; \; \phantom{9} \; \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    And since 122 is smaller than 721, the 7 was the correct choice. Bring down the 4:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2\;\;7\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 721 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;1\;\; 9 \; \; 5 \; \; 8\; \; 9\;\;4\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;1 \; \; 4 \;\;4\;\;2\;} \; \phantom{2}\;\; \phantom{6}\;\amp \\ 5 \;\; 1 \; \; 6 \;\;9\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ \underline{5\;\;0\;\; 4 \; \; 7 \; } \; \phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 1\;\;2 \; \; 2 \; \; 4 \; \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    And we look for \(1224 \div 721\) and this is 1. Recall that above we did \(2 \times 721 = 1442\) which is larger than 1224.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2\;\;7\;\;1 \; \amp \\ 721 \; )\!\!\overline{\;\;1\;\; 9 \; \; 5 \; \; 8\; \; 9\;\;4\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;1 \; \; 4 \;\;4\;\;2\;} \; \phantom{2}\;\; \phantom{6}\;\amp \\ 5 \;\; 1 \; \; 6 \;\;9\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ \underline{5\;\;0\;\; 4 \; \; 7 \; } \; \phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 1\;\;2 \; \; 2 \; \; 4 \; \amp \\ \underline{7 \;\; 2 \;\; 1\;} \amp \\ 5\;\;0\;\;3\; \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This shows that \(195894 \div 721 = 271 \; R\; 503\)

    Subsection 5.4.5 Base-5 Long Division

    We lastly look at base-5 long division. We’ll start by doing a chip model of a division problem. Consider \(414_5\div 3_5\text{.}\) We can write this as
    🔗
    Since we are dividing these into 3 groups, we’ll make 3 rows in the chart to move the chips:
    🔗
    We move 3 of the chips in the top row of the 25s column down:
    🔗
    For the last chip in the top row of the 25s column, we unbundle (as 5 five-chips) and spread them evenly
    🔗
    Next, we move the remaining fives chips down below to make the rows even:
    🔗
    And lastly, we move 3 of the ones into the rows evenly:
    🔗
    And since each row now has 1 quarter (25), 2 nickels (5s) and 1 penny (1s) with 1 penny leftover this shows that
    \begin{equation*} 414_5 \div 3_5 = 121_5 \; R \; 1_5 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Let’s look at this in long division style. First, as you probably realize, it is key to have 1-digit multiplication. Recall the base-5 multiplication table:
    🔗
    \(\times\) 0 1 2 3 4
    0 0 0 0 0 0
    1 0 1 2 3 4
    2 0 2 4 11 14
    3 0 3 11 14 22
    4 0 4 13 22 31
    We’ll write down the steps to solve \(414_5\div 3_5\) in a manner similar to that for base-10:
    🔗
    1. Start with the long division for \(414_5\div 3_5\) or
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \phantom{2}\;\;\phantom{1}\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 3\; \overline{)\;4\;\; 1 \; \; 4 \;} \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      The first step will be to determine \(4 \div 3\text{,}\) which has a quotient of 1, so
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 1\;\;\phantom{1}\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 3\; \overline{)\;4\;\; 1 \; \; 4 \;} \amp \\ \underline{3\;}\;\phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ 1 \; \;\phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      and then we bring the 1 from the dividend down:
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 1\;\;\phantom{1}\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 3\; \overline{)\;4\;\; 1 \; \; 4 \;} \amp \\ \underline{3\;}\;\phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ 1 \; \;1\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Next, find \(11 \div 3\) (base-5) which looking at the multiplication table is 2. Add that to the top of the long division and add the product \(3 \times 2 = 11\) and subtract:
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 1\;\;2\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 3\; \overline{)\;4\;\; 1 \; \; 4 \;} \amp \\ \underline{3\;}\;\phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ 1 \; \;1\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ \underline{1 \; \;1\;\;\phantom{0}}\; \amp \\ 0 \; \; \phantom{4} \; \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      Next, bring the 4 down from the dividend:
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 1\;\;2\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 3\; \overline{)\;4\;\; 1 \; \; 4 \;} \amp \\ \underline{3\;}\;\phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ 1 \; \;1\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ \underline{1 \; \;1\;\;\phantom{0}}\; \amp \\ 0 \; \; 4 \; \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Lastly, do the division \(4 \div 3\text{,}\) which again is 1:
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 1\;\;2\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 3\; \overline{)\;4\;\; 1 \; \; 4 \;} \amp \\ \underline{3\;}\;\phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ 1 \; \;1\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ \underline{1 \; \;1\;\;\phantom{0}}\; \amp \\ 0 \; \; 4 \; \amp \\ \underline{\;3 \;} \amp \\ 1 \; \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Showing the results are \(414_5 \div 3_5 = 121_5 \; R \; 1_5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We now show how to think about performing long division with a 2-digit divisor:
    🔗

    Example 5.4.6.

    Find \(4241_5\div 21_5\) using long division:
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, let’s make this a bit easier and build a table of products of 21 which can be fairly easier found by successive addition:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 1 \times 21 \amp = 21 \amp 2 \times 21 \amp = 42 \amp 3 \times 21 \amp = 113 \amp 4 \times 21 \amp = 134 \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Start the long division:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \phantom{1}\;\;\phantom{2}\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 21\; \overline{)\;4\;\; 3 \; \; 4 \;\; 1\;} \amp \\ \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Our first task is \(43 \div 21\text{,}\) which from the table is \(2\) ( \(43\) is between 42 and 113, so round down):
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2\;\;\phantom{2}\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 21\; \overline{)\;4\;\; 3 \; \; 4 \;\; 1\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;4\;\;2\;}\;\phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ 1 \; \; \phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    bring the 4 down from the dividend:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2\;\;\phantom{2}\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 21\; \overline{)\;4\;\; 3 \; \; 4 \;\; 1\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;4\;\;2\;}\;\phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ 1 \; \; 4\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and then we do \(14 \div 21\text{,}\) which is 0, so include that above the line and bring down the 1 from the dividend:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2\;\;0\;\;\phantom{0} \; \amp \\ 21\; \overline{)\;4\;\; 3 \; \; 4 \;\; 1\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;4\;\;2\;}\;\phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ 1 \; \; 4\;\;1\; \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and now we do \(141 \div 21\text{,}\) which from the table is 4:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2\;\;0\;\;4 \; \amp \\ 21\; \overline{)\;4\;\; 3 \; \; 4 \;\; 1\;} \amp \\ \underline{\;4\;\;2\;}\;\phantom{0}\;\;\phantom{0}\; \amp \\ 1 \; \; 4\;\;1\; \amp \\ \underline{\; 1 \; \; 3 \; \; 4 \; } \amp \\ 2 \; \amp \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    where recall that the subtraction \(141-134=2\) is in base- \(5\text{.}\) The result shows that \(4341_5\div 21_{\text{5}}= 204_{\text{5}}\; R \; 2_5\text{.}\)
