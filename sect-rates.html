    Section 8.4 Rates, Speed, and Arithmetic with Units

    Objectives

    • Understand the notion of a rate.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Find the rate given two quantities.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use rates in solving problems.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Solving Joint Work Rate Problems.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    How would you solve: If you drive 30 miles per hour, how far do you drive in 2 hours?.
    🔗
    This is an example of a rate problem with the rate 30 miles per hour. Hopefully this problem is intuitive enough to realize the answer is 60 miles (multiply 30 and 2). But other rate problems might require dividing the numbers. How do you know what is appropriate?
    🔗
    The problems in this section will use the Dimensional Analysis technique from Section 8.3.
    🔗

    Subsection 8.4.1 Rates

    With the example above, you might remember that a rate tells you how fast (or slow) something is occurring in that it is some quantity per some unit of time. The example above is miles per hour, but other rates could in feet per second, or cupcakes per week or gallons per minute.
    🔗

    Example 8.4.1.

    Janette can ride her bicycle 3 miles in 20 minutes. What is her speed (rate) is miles per minute? In miles per hour?
    🔗
    🔗
    However, we’re going to broaden this idea of rate requiring a unit of time to the following:
    🔗

    Definition 8.4.2. Rate.

    A rate is a quotient of two quantities with different specified units.
    🔗
    🔗
    Using this definition of rate, 10 feet per second, 500 cupcakes per week and 15 gallons per minutes all fit this definition, however, the following work as well:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{10\text{ yd$^{2}$}}{4\text{ shirts}}, \quad \frac{6 \text{ bags of concrete}}{15\text{ cubic feet}}, \quad \frac{1 \text{ pound of sugar}}{2 \text{ gallons}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We desire to solve problems that have rates that fit these as well.
    🔗

    Note 8.4.3. Solving Problems with Rates.

    Consider a word problem in which units play a role. There are generally two different pieces of information in the problem.
    1. rates which are often written as sentences that given how a unit represents some other unit.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. given information, which is often in the question of the problem. This should have a unit as well.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    To solve this we will use Dimensional Analysis like the previous section as:
    1. Write all rates as fractions with the units.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Start with the given information (a number with a unit).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Multiply the quantity in #2 by rates, to ensure that the units cancel resulting in the correct unit in the answer.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The steps above probably seem quite vague, so we spend the rest of this section covering examples in detail.
    🔗

    Example 8.4.4.

    If you drive 30 miles per hour, how far do you drive in 2 hours?
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, we need to identify the rates and the given information. In this case, the rate is 30 miles per hour and is written:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{30\text{ miles}}{1\text{ hour}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The given information is 2 hours. To solve this, we start with this quantity and multiply by rates:
    \begin{equation*} 2\cancel{\text{ hours}}\times \frac{30\text{ miles}}{1\cancel{\text{ hour}}}= 60\text{ miles} \end{equation*}
    where it is important that the unit “hours” cancels.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Since rates include units, sometimes it’s important to do unit conversion as well. The next couple of examples show this.
    🔗

    Example 8.4.5.

    You are on a road trip and are driving 40 miles per hour.
    🔗
    (a)
    How far do you drive in 15 minutes?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Clearly, the main rate given in the problem has the relationship:
    \begin{equation*} 1\text{ hour} = 40\text{ miles} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    However if we start with the given information of 15 minutes, the rate doesn’t have minutes, so we need the conversion 1 hour = 60 minutes as well
    \begin{equation*} 15\cancel{\text{ minutes}}\times \frac{1\cancel{\text{ hour}}}{60\cancel{\text{ minutes}}}\times \frac{40\text{ miles}}{1\cancel{\text{ hour}}}= \frac{15 \times 40}{60}\text{ miles} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    And the units cancel correctly, but you show also remember from Section 7.3 that an efficient way to find the answer is first cancel common terms and
    \begin{equation*} \frac{15 \times 40}{60}\text{ miles}= \frac{15 \times 40}{15 \times 4}\text{ miles}= \frac{ 40}{4}\text{ miles}= 10 \text{ miles} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So you drive 10 miles in 15 minutes.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    How long would it take you to drive 100 miles?
    🔗
    Solution.
    For the second question of how long it would take to drive 100 miles, this is the given information, so we start with this and the rate.
    \begin{equation*} 100\cancel{\text{ miles}}\times \frac{1\text{ hour}}{40\cancel{\text{ miles}}} \end{equation*}
    where 1) notice that the units cancel giving the answer in hours and 2) we have used the reciprocal of the rate as given in the problem to get the units to cancel.
    🔗
    The answer is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{100}{40}\text{ miles} = \frac{10}{4}\text{ miles}= \frac{5}{2}\text{ miles} \end{equation*}
    or probably easier to say \(2 \frac{1}{2}\) hours.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.4.6.

    A bakery makes 60 cupcakes in an hour. The bakery is open 8 hours each day. How many cupcakes can they make in 1 week?
    🔗
    Solution.
    There are two rates in this problem. We can write them as
    \begin{equation*} 60\text{ cupcakes} = 1\text{ hour} \qquad 8\text{ hours} = 1\text{ day} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The starting info is 1 week and from the question, we’re looking for the unit of “cupcakes”. Before we begin, we note that the starting unit of week is not in either of the rates, so we will need the conversion \(1\text{ week} = 7\text{ days}\) as well.
    \begin{equation*} 1\cancel{\text{ week}}\times \frac{7\cancel{\text{ days}}}{1\cancel{\text{ week}}}\times \frac{8\cancel{\text{ hours}}}{1\cancel{\text{ day}}}\times \frac{60\text{ cupcakes}}{1\cancel{\text{ hour}}}= 7 \times 8 \times 60 \text{ cupcakes} \end{equation*}
    and some mental math or using the algorithm finishes the problem. The bakery can make 3360 cupcakes in a week.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.4.7.

    A hose can fill a 5 gallon bucket in 15 minutes. In how many hours, will it take the hose to fill a 600 gallon pool?
    🔗
    Solution.
    In this case we start with 600 gallons and convert to hours. There is also one rate given in the problem.
    \begin{equation*} 5\text{ gal} = 15 \text{ min}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Note that we need a conversion from minutes to hours so will also need to use \(1\text{ hour} = 60\text{ min}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} 600\cancel{\text{ gallons}}\times \frac{15\cancel{\text{ min.}}}{5\cancel{\text{ gallon}}}\times \frac{1\text{ hr}}{60\cancel{\text{ min}}} \end{equation*}
    and again, use factoring within the fraction to simplify the final operation
    \begin{equation*} \frac{600 \times 15 \times 1}{5 \times 60}= \frac{\cancel{60}\times 10 \times \cancel{5}\times 3}{\cancel{5} \times \cancel{60}}= 30 \end{equation*}
    which shows that it will take 30 hours to fill the pool.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The previous examples used rates with time, however as we noted, any types can work as the next example shows.
    🔗

    Example 8.4.8.

    A pattern for a shirt uses fabric. 4 shirts require 10 yd \(^{2}\) of fabric. How many shirts can be made with 60 yd \(^{2}\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, note that the rate in this problem is
    \begin{equation*} 10 \text{yd}^{2} = 4\text{ shirts} \end{equation*}
    and we start with 60 yd \(^{2}\) and convert to the unit “shirts”.
    \begin{equation*} 60\cancel{\text{ yd$^{2}$}}\times \frac{4\text{ shirts}}{10\cancel{\text{ yd$^{2}$}}}= \frac{240}{10}\text{ shirts}= 24\text{ shirts} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.4.9.

    A brine mixture for a Thanksgiving turkey calls for 1/3 cup of salt for every quart of water. How much salt is needed for a 4 gallon brine?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The primary rate from the problem is \(\frac{1}{3}\text{ cup salt} = 1 \text{ quart water}\text{.}\) Note even though both are volume measure, the quantity of salt or water is needed with the unit.
    🔗
    Also, we will need the volume conversion of \(4\text{ quart} = 1\text{ gallon}\text{,}\) then start with the given quantity of \(4\text{ gallons water}\) and then use the rates in the proper form:
    \begin{align*} A \amp = 4\cancel{\text{ gallons water}} \times \frac{4\cancel{\text{ quarts water}}}{1\cancel{\text{ gallon water}}} \times \frac{1/3\text{ cup salt}}{1\cancel{\text{ quart water}}} \\ \amp = \frac{16}{3} \text{ cups salt} \end{align*}
    or as a mixed number, \(5 \frac{1}{3}\) cups salt.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.4.2 Joint Work Rate Problems

    Another common problem involving rates is that of combining them, such as if two people are doing the same job, how long will it take if they work together? Consider the following example:
    🔗

    Example 8.4.10.

    Paul can do a job in 2 hours, whereas Quinn can do the same job in 1 hour. How long will it take for them to do the job together?
    🔗
    🔗
    Maybe these don’t seem like rates, but we can write them as
    \begin{align*} \text{Paul:}\amp \qquad \text{1 job} = \text{2 hours} \\ \text{Quinn:}\amp \qquad \text{1 job} =\text{1 hour} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Since we want to combine these, it seems natural to add them, but since rates work as two possible fractions, let’s look at both of these.
    🔗
    Option A
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\text{1 job}}{\text{2 hours}} +\frac{\text{1 job}}{\text{1 hour}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Option B:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\text{2 hours}}{\text{1 job}} +\frac{\text{1 hour}}{\text{1 job}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and since the units are equal for the two rates (fraction), it’s fine to add them, but remember to add them as fractions. Finding the resulting rate is:
    🔗
    Option A:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\text{1 job}}{\text{2 hours}} +\frac{\text{1 job}}{\text{1 hour}} = \biggl( \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{1} \biggr) \frac{\text{job}}{\text{hour}} = \biggl( \frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{2} \biggr) \frac{\text{job}}{\text{hour}} = \frac{3}{2} \frac{\text{jobs}}{\text{hour}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Option B:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\text{2 hours}}{\text{1 job}} +\frac{\text{1 hour}}{\text{1 job}} = \frac{3\text{ hours}}{1 \text{ job}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Which is the correct combined rates? Let’s think about it a bit. It should be that if two people do a job, that together will be faster than either along. In Option A, they can do \(\frac{3}{2}\) jobs (or \(1\frac{1}{2}\) jobs) in one hour, which is faster than either by themselves. In Option B, the will take 3 hours to do the job, which is slower. Clearly Option A is correct.
    🔗
    Finally, to finish the problem, we start with the unit 1 job and find the amount of time as we have done with other problems in this section.
    \begin{equation*} 1\cancel{\text{ job}}\times \frac{2\cancel{\text{ hours}}}{3\cancel{\text{ jobs}}}\times \frac{60\text{ min.}}{1\cancel{\text{ hour}}}= \frac{120}{3}\text{ minutes}= 40\text{ minutes} \end{equation*}
    where we have also introduce the unit conversion of hours to minutes.
    🔗
    Since there are two ways of adding rates (either the rates as given or flipped), here’s a good rule of thumb.
    🔗

    Note 8.4.11. Combining Rates.

    If there are two rates that are to be combined in the sense of completing a task, add the rates with the time unit in the denominator.
    🔗
    🔗
    This should help us in combining rates and we’ll see this in the following example.
    🔗

    Example 8.4.12.

    Brandon can shovel a driveway in 30 minutes. Eric can shovel the same driveway in 60 minutes. If they work together, how long will it take them?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The rates in this problem are
    \begin{equation*} 1 \text{~driveway} = 30 \text{~minutes}, \qquad 1 \text{~driveway}= 60 \text{~minutes} \end{equation*}
    and if we take the rule of thumb above, we’ll add them with the minutes in the denominator.
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1 \text{~driveway}}{30 \text{~minutes}}+ \frac{1 \text{~driveway}}{60 \text{~minutes}}= \frac{3 \text{~driveways}}{60 \text{~minutes}}= \frac{1 \text{~driveway}}{20 \text{~minutes}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Lastly, we’ll start with 1 driveway and convert to minutes:
    \begin{equation*} 1\cancel{\text{ driveway}}\times \frac{20 \text{ minutes}}{1 \cancel{\text{ driveway}}}= 20\text{ minutes} \end{equation*}
    so it will take them 20 minutes to shovel the driveway.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    This last example shows that this also works for more than 2 rates.
    🔗

    Example 8.4.13.

    Bernard can paint a house in 30 hours. Cindy can paint the same house in 20 hours and Declan can do it in 40 hours. How long will it take to paint the house together?
    🔗
    Solution.
    In this case, there are three rates:
    \begin{equation*} 1\text{ house} = 30\text{ hours}, \qquad 1\text{ house} = 20\text{ hours}, \qquad 1\text{ house} = 40\text{ hours}, \end{equation*}
    and using the rate combining rule of thumb we’ll add them with hours in the denominator:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1\text{ house}}{30\text{ hours}}+\frac{1\text{ house}}{20\text{ hours}}+ \frac{1\text{ house}}{40\text{ hours}} = \biggl(\frac{1}{30}+ \frac{1}{20}+ \frac{1}{40}\biggr) \frac{\text{house}}{\text{hour}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Recall that to make this problem easier use the LCM of these three numbers, or 120
    \begin{align*} \phantom{\frac{1\text{ house}}{30\text{ hours}}+\frac{1\text{ house}}{20\text{ hours}}+ \frac{1\text{ house}}{40\text{ hours}}}\amp = \biggl(\frac{4}{120}+ \frac{6}{120}+ \frac{3}{120}\biggr) \frac{\text{house}}{\text{hour}} \\ \amp = \frac{13}{120}\frac{\text{house}}{\text{hour}} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Finally, to answer the question, we’ll start with 1 house and multiply by the rate:
    \begin{equation*} 1\cancel{\text{ house}}\times \frac{120 \text{ hour}}{13\cancel{\text{ house}}} \end{equation*}
    and converting this to a mixed number, it will take them \(9\frac{3}{13}\) hours to paint the house.
