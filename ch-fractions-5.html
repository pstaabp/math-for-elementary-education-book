    Section 7.3 Multiplication of Fractions

    Multiplication of fractions is done, like much of mathematics in stages, building on what is known. We will first look at a whole numbers times a fraction, move to multiplication of proper fractions and last speak to multiplication of mixed numbers or improper fractions.
    🔗
    As we will see, diagrams will play a huge role, both grid and bar diagrams are generally the most helpful.
    🔗

    Subsection 7.3.1 Multiplication of a whole number and a fraction

    If we return to the interpretation of multiplication, \(a \times b\) is the number \(b\) added to itself \(a\) times. It can also be interpreted as \(a\) groups of items each size \(b\) and \(a\) jumps on the number line of size \(b\text{.}\) We can think of each of these interpretations. For example, consider \(4 \times \frac{1}{5}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} 4 \times \frac{1}{5} = \frac{1}{5}+ \frac{1}{5}+ \frac{1}{5}+ \frac{1}{5}= \frac{4}{5}\\ \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A nice way to think about a whole number times a fraction is to use the number like to do the whole number of hops of size the fraction. Here’s \(4 \times \frac{1}{5}\text{:}\)
    🔗
    This shows that \(4 \times \dfrac{1}{5} = \dfrac{4}{5} \text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.3.2 Fraction times a whole number

    We can use a grid model to show multiplication, but you need to be careful with it. Consider \(4 \times \frac{1}{5}\text{.}\) The fraction \(\frac{1}{5}\) can be a unit where 1 of 5 parts is shaded. In the other direction the 4 can just be 4 units as shown below:
    🔗
    There are 4 shaded squares and note that a single square is \(1/5\text{,}\) so this shows that \(4 \times \frac{1}{5} = \frac{4}{5}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This could also be interpreted as
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{5} \times 4 = \frac{4}{5} \end{equation*}
    and noting that this would show that
    \begin{equation*} 4 \times \frac{1}{5} = \frac{1}{5} \times 4 \end{equation*}
    so the commutative property of integers also seems to hold for fractions as well.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.3.3 Fraction times a fraction

    Our next step in fractionland is a fraction times a fraction (we’ll just consider proper fractions right now). As an example, let’s look at
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{4}\times \frac{3}{5} \end{equation*}
    and we’ll interpret this as \(\frac{1}{4}\) of \(\frac{3}{5}\text{.}\) Let’s think of this as a diagram like where we’ll break up into 5 equal pieces and shade three of them:
    🔗
    and the shaded area represents \(\frac{3}{5}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since we seek to find \(\frac{1}{4}\) of this, then we also break the unit into fourths in the other direction:
    🔗
    and each box in the grid above is \(\dfrac{1}{4 \times 5}= \dfrac{1}{20}\text{.}\) The red shaded area represents the answer or \(\frac{3}{20}\text{.}\) This shows that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{4}\times \frac{3}{5}= \frac{3}{20} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Property 7.3.1. Fraction Multiplication.

    Let \(a,b,c\) and \(d\) be positive whole numbers, then
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a}{b}\cdot \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \cdot c}{b \cdot d} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.3.2.

    \begin{equation*} \frac{2}{3}\cdot \frac{4}{5} = \frac{ 2 \cdot 4}{3 \cdot 5}= \frac{8}{15} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 7.3.3.

    Notice that a common denominator is not needed for multiplication, only for addition and subtraction.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.3.4 Mixed number times mixed number

    Similar to that of addition and subtraction, we can multiply mixed numbers. However, it is quite difficult to do so directly because of how a mixed number is interpreted. For example
    \begin{equation*} 3 \dfrac{1}{3}\times 2 \dfrac{2}{5} \end{equation*}
    can be written as
    \begin{equation*} \bigg(3 + \frac{1}{3}\biggr) \times \biggl( 2 + \frac{2}{5}\biggr) \end{equation*}
    and you would have to apply the distributive properties. Instead, a much easier way to convert to improper numbers first as is shown in the next example.
    🔗

    Example 7.3.4.

    Multiplying Mixed Numbers
    🔗
    Find \(3\frac{1}{3}\times 2\frac{3}{5}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, we can write \(3\frac{1}{3}= \frac{10}{3}\) and \(2\frac{3}{5}= \frac{13}{5}\) and then
    \begin{align*} 3 \dfrac{1}{3}\times 2 \dfrac{3}{5} \amp = \frac{10}{3}\times \frac{13}{5} \\ \amp = \frac{10 \times 13}{3 \times 5}= \frac{2 \times \cancel{5} \times 13 }{3 \times \cancel{5}} \\ \amp = \frac{2 \times 13}{3}= \frac{26}{3} \end{align*}
    where the last step used equivalent fractions to reduce it.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.3.5 Factoring Fractions and Canceling

    The previous example performed some cancelling to reduce the answer. There are often two different ways to perform a multiplication for example we find
    \begin{equation*} \frac{5}{18} \times \frac{3}{20} \end{equation*}
    using two different techniques.
    🔗
    No factoring
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{5}{18}\times \frac{3}{20} = \frac{5 \times 3}{18 \times 20}= \frac{15}{360}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If you you need to reduce the fraction and generally that is the best, then at this step you’ll need to note that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{15}{360} = \frac{15}{15 \cdot 24} = \frac{1}{24} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Reduce first
    🔗
    Instead, if we reduce the fraction before multiplying
    \begin{equation*} \frac{5}{18}\times \frac{3}{20} = \frac{5 \times 3}{18 \times 20}= \frac{\cancel{5} \times \cancel{3}}{6 \times \cancel{3} \times \cancel{5} \times 4}= \frac{1}{24} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Almost always, it’s easier to perform reducing the fraction before multiplying. This is because the resulting number is smaller.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.3.6 Word Problems

    The following are two different word problems involving multiplication with fractions.
    🔗

    Example 7.3.5.

    Develop a Teacher’s solution of the following: Mrs. Baker had \(\frac{2}{3}\) lb of sugar. She used \(\frac{3}{4}\) of it in a recipe. How much sugar did she use?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The answer is \(\frac{3}{4}\) of \(\frac{2}{3}\) as this is most easily shown as the following bar diagram:
    🔗
    The amount of sugar used is the product of \(\frac{3}{4}\) and \(\frac{2}{3}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{3}{4}\times \frac{2}{3}= \frac{3 \times 2}{4 \times 3}= \frac{6}{12}= \frac{1}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Mrs. Baker used \(\frac{1}{2}\) of a pound of sugar.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
